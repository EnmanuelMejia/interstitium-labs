OBJECTIVES:

- SLO targets and measurement windows

- Budget-exhaustion consequences

--- NOTE: How Interstitium Labs teaches this ---
The vocabulary every SRE interview runs on: what to measure, what target to promise, and what happens when the budget burns.

--- NOTE: Sources ---
- **Google SRE Books** (https://sre.google/) — The SRE book and workbook: SLIs/SLOs, incidents, and reliability math, from the source.
- **Prometheus Documentation** (https://prometheus.io/docs/) — Metrics, PromQL, and alerting references for the observability spine.

--- CARDS ---
What are the four golden signals? ||| Latency, traffic, errors, saturation — the minimum dashboard for any service. In an interview, reach for them first: ‘I’d check the four golden signals’ is the SRE equivalent of ‘let me reproduce it.’
SLI vs SLO vs SLA? ||| SLI: the measured indicator (e.g. successful requests / total). SLO: the target on the SLI (99.9% over 30 days). SLA: the contract with consequences. The interview line: ‘SLIs are facts, SLOs are promises, SLAs are contracts.’
How do error budgets govern releases? ||| Budget remaining → ship freely. Budget exhausted → freeze risky changes until reliability recovers. It replaces arguments about ‘are we reliable enough’ with arithmetic — the interview line: ‘the budget turns reliability into a joint product decision.’
How do you explain burn-rate alerting? ||| Instead of alerting on raw error counts, alert on how fast the error budget is being consumed relative to the SLO window: fast-burn (page now) vs slow-burn (ticket). Multiwindow alerts catch both without paging on blips.
How do you measure SLIs with Prometheus? ||| Counters for requests and errors (rate over windows), histograms/summaries for latency percentiles, recording rules to precompute SLI ratios, and burn-rate alert rules on top. The interview line: ‘the SLI is a PromQL expression with an SLO attached.’

--- QUIZ ---
Q: A latency SLI in Prometheus is typically built from:
 choices: ['Gauges only', 'Histogram buckets — enabling accurate percentile computation over windows', 'Logs', 'Traces']
 answer: 1 explain: Histograms preserve the distribution: any percentile over any window is computable.

Q: Burn-rate alerting’s advantage over static error thresholds is:
 choices: ['Simpler config', 'It measures budget consumption relative to the SLO — paging only when the objective is actually at risk', 'Fewer metrics', 'No Prometheus needed']
 answer: 1 explain: Thresholds don’t know your SLO; burn rate ties paging directly to objective risk.

Q: When the error budget is exhausted, the correct policy is:
 choices: ['Ship faster', 'Freeze or slow risky releases until the budget recovers', 'Lower the SLO', 'Ignore it']
 answer: 1 explain: The budget is the agreed tradeoff dial: spending it all means reliability work comes first.

Q: An error budget is:
 choices: ['The on-call schedule', '1 − SLO: the allowed failure rate, spent deliberately on releases and risk', 'A financial budget', 'The alert threshold']
 answer: 1 explain: 99.9% SLO → 0.1% budget: reliability as a spendable resource balancing velocity and stability.

--- NOTE: Sources ---

- **CISA Resources & Tools** (https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools) — US government guidance on hardening, incident response, and best practice.

--- CARDS ---
Walk the incident lifecycle. ||| Detect (alerts, user reports) → respond (declare, assemble, IC takes command) → mitigate (stop the bleeding — rollback, shed load) → resolve → learn (blameless postmortem). The interview line: ‘mitigate first, understand second.’
What are the incident command roles? ||| Incident Commander (decides, coordinates — doesn’t debug), Communications (status updates, stakeholders), Scribe (timeline), plus subject-matter experts debugging. The IC’s job is the incident, not the keyboard.
How do you discuss defect lifecycle and severity? ||| Lifecycle: new → triaged → in progress → fixed → verified → closed (reopened if it regresses). Severity (impact) vs priority (fix order) are different axes — a cosmetic bug on the checkout button can be high priority. The interview line: ‘severity is impact; priority is scheduling.’
How do you communicate during an outage? ||| One channel (#incident), regular cadence (every 15–30 min even with ‘no update’), impact-first language (who’s affected, what we’re doing, when’s the next update), and a single comms owner. The interview line: ‘silence during an outage is a second outage.’
What makes a postmortem blameless and useful? ||| Blameless: systems and process, never people — or nobody will write honestly. Structure: timeline, impact, root causes (plural — ‘five whys’), action items with owners and dates. The interview line: ‘the postmortem’s value is the action items, not the document.’

--- QUIZ ---
Q: An ML system design that only covers the model is weak because:
 choices: ['Models don’t matter', 'Production value lives in data, serving, and monitoring — the model is one piece', 'It’s too short', 'Interviewers are bored']
 answer: 1 explain: Most ML failures are systems failures: skew, drift, latency — not the algorithm.

Q: Blameless postmortems work because:
 choices: ['They’re shorter', 'Removing blame gets honest timelines — people report what really happened', 'They skip action items', 'They’re optional']
 answer: 1 explain: Honest data requires psychological safety; blame produces cover stories instead of root causes.

Q: Status updates during an outage should lead with:
 choices: ['Technical details', 'Customer impact: who’s affected and what’s being done', 'Blame', 'The timeline']
 answer: 1 explain: Stakeholders need impact and next-update time; deep technical detail follows for those who need it.

Q: Severity vs priority differ in that:
 choices: ['They’re synonyms', 'Severity = impact on users; priority = order of fixing (business decision)', 'Priority is technical', 'Severity is decided by testers only']
 answer: 1 explain: A severe bug in a deprecated feature can be low priority; a trivial bug blocking launch is high priority.

--- NOTE: Sources ---

- **OpenTelemetry Documentation** (https://opentelemetry.io/docs/) — Vendor-neutral tracing, metrics, and logs instrumentation guides.

--- CARDS ---
Metrics, logs, traces — the three pillars in an SRE interview? ||| Metrics: numbers over time (alerts, trends). Logs: discrete events with context (the why). Traces: request journeys across services (the where). The interview line: ‘metrics tell me something’s wrong, traces show me where, logs tell me why.’
What are the four golden signals? ||| Latency, traffic, errors, saturation — the minimum dashboard for any service. In an interview, reach for them first: ‘I’d check the four golden signals’ is the SRE equivalent of ‘let me reproduce it.’
USE vs RED methods? ||| USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) for resources — ‘is the box healthy.’ RED (Rate, Errors, Duration) for services — ‘is the service healthy.’ In an interview, name which lens fits: debugging a host → USE; debugging an API → RED.
Why alert on symptoms, not causes? ||| Users feel symptoms (latency, error rate); causes are legion and change. Symptom alerts (burn rate, golden signals) page on user impact; cause-based alerts (‘disk 90%’) page on things that may not matter — alert fatigue follows.
What makes a dashboard that ‘answers questions’? ||| Golden signals up top, drill-down from symptom to cause, time-range comparisons, and annotations for deploys — organized by the questions on-call asks at 3am, not by data source. The interview line: ‘a dashboard is a UI for incident questions.’

--- QUIZ ---

 choices: ['It’s PII only', 'Each unique value creates a new time series — cardinality explosion', 'It’s slow to type', 'Labels can’t be strings']
 answer: 1 explain: Unbounded label values × metrics = millions of series; memory and query costs follow.

Q: Deploy annotations on dashboards help because:
 choices: ['They look nice', 'They correlate behavior changes with releases — the first suspect in any regression', 'They’re required', 'They speed queries']
 answer: 1 explain: ‘What changed?’ is the first incident question; annotations answer it at a glance.

Q: Alerting on symptoms means paging when:
 choices: ['A disk hits 90%', 'User-facing indicators (latency/errors) breach SLO — regardless of underlying cause', 'CPU spikes briefly', 'A deploy happens']
 answer: 1 explain: Pages should mean user impact; cause metrics are for dashboards and diagnosis, not paging.

Q: The RED method suits:
 choices: ['Disk debugging', 'Request-driven services: rate, errors, duration', 'Network cables', 'CPU fans']
 answer: 1 explain: RED describes service behavior from the requester’s view; USE describes resource internals.

--- NOTE: How Interstitium Labs teaches this ---

--- NOTE: Sources ---
- **Google SRE Books** (https://sre.google/) — The SRE book and workbook: SLIs/SLOs, incidents, and reliability math, from the source.
- **Kubernetes Documentation** (https://kubernetes.io/docs/) — Core objects, networking, and failure-mode references for orchestration modules.

--- CARDS ---
How do you forecast demand for capacity planning? ||| Historical growth rates plus known events (launches, seasonality, marketing), expressed as ranges not points, translated through the capacity model (requests → resources). The interview line: ‘forecasts are ranges with assumptions, reviewed on a cadence.’
k6 vs JMeter — how do you compare them? ||| k6: JS-scripted, developer-friendly, great for CI-integrated load tests with thresholds as gates. JMeter: GUI-driven, mature, huge protocol support, heavier. The interview line: ‘k6 for code-reviewed load tests in the pipeline; JMeter where the team already lives in it.’
HPA vs VPA? ||| HPA: more pods when load rises (horizontal — the cloud-native default). VPA: bigger pods (vertical — for workloads that can’t scale out, needs restarts). The interview caveat: autoscaling isn’t instant — cold starts and scaling lag mean you still need headroom.
What’s the utilization wall? ||| Latency degrades nonlinearly as utilization approaches 100% (queueing theory): a system at 90%+ has no headroom for bursts — the ‘wall’ where small load increases cause large latency spikes. The interview line: ‘I plan headroom against the wall, not against the average.’
What’s the N+1 query problem and its fix? ||| Loading N parents then one query per parent’s children: N+1 round trips. The fix is eager loading — a JOIN or batched second query (and ORMs have explicit eager-loading APIs). Name it when you see loops over queries.

--- QUIZ ---
Q: Spot instances fit batch workloads because:
 choices: ['They’re faster', 'Interruption-tolerant work buys spare capacity at deep discounts', 'They’re reliable', 'No setup']
 answer: 1 explain: Price follows preemptibility; batch work that checkpoints doesn’t care.

Q: Eager loading fixes N+1 by:
 choices: ['Adding more indexes', 'Fetching related rows in bulk (JOIN or batched query) instead of one query per parent', 'Caching the database', 'Using NoSQL']
 answer: 1 explain: One bulk fetch replaces N per-row queries; round trips collapse from N+1 to ~2.

Q: Running a service at 95% CPU is risky because:
 choices: ['It’s inefficient', 'Queueing effects make latency spike nonlinearly near saturation — no burst headroom', 'It wastes power', 'CPUs degrade']
 answer: 1 explain: Near 100% utilization, tiny load increases produce huge queueing delays.

Q: Horizontal Pod Autoscaler reacts to load by:
 choices: ['Increasing pod CPU limits', 'Adding more pod replicas', 'Restarting nodes', 'Throttling traffic']
 answer: 1 explain: HPA scales replica count on metrics; VPA resizes individual pods.

--- CARDS ---
How do you design for multi-region in an interview? ||| Active-passive (one serves, one stands by) vs active-active (both serve — needs conflict resolution); data replication with RPO/RTO stated; DNS or anycast failover; and the honest cost: complexity and consistency tradeoffs multiply. The interview line: ‘multi-region is a business decision with an engineering price.’
What is backpressure, and where do you put it? ||| When consumers are slower than producers, pressure must go somewhere: bounded queues that block/shed, reactive streams with demand signaling, or Kafka consumer-lag alerting. The interview point: an unbounded queue is a deferred outage.
How do caching layers behave under failure? ||| Cache stampedes on cold start, thundering herd on hot-key expiry, and the deadly combo: cache down → DB overload → cascading failure. Defenses: request coalescing, staggered TTLs, circuit breakers, and graceful degradation (serve stale while revalidating).
What’s load shedding, and when do you use it? ||| Deliberately dropping low-priority work to protect the core: priority queues, shedding non-critical traffic at the edge, degrading features gracefully. The interview line: ‘shedding is a choice — uncontrolled collapse is not.’
What’s the standard system-design walkthrough? ||| Requirements → estimation → API → data model → happy-path design → scaling/bottlenecks → failure modes → tradeoffs summary. Drive with questions, think aloud, and treat the interviewer as a collaborator, not an examiner.

Q: Load shedding differs from a cascading failure in that it’s:
 choices: ['Faster', 'Deliberate and prioritized — you choose what degrades instead of everything failing', 'Automatic only', 'A bug']
 answer: 1 explain: Controlled degradation preserves the critical path; cascades take everything down.

Q: Serving stale cache during an origin outage is:

Q: A bounded queue with a drop-or-block policy implements:

 answer: 1 explain: Bounded buffers propagate slowness upstream instead of growing memory until collapse.

What’s the case for trunk-based development? ||| Small, frequent merges to main behind feature flags: integration pain drops with merge frequency, main stays releasable, and long-lived branch divergence dies. The interview line: ‘branches are inventory — trunk-based keeps inventory near zero.’
What belongs in CI stages, and in what order? ||| Fast-first: lint and unit tests (minutes), then build, integration tests, security scans, artifact publish. The interview line: ‘fail fast and cheap — a 40-minute pipeline that fails at minute 39 teaches nothing quickly.’

--- CARDS ---
Declarative vs imperative infrastructure? ||| Declarative: state the desired end state, the tool converges (Terraform, K8s manifests) — idempotent and auditable. Imperative: script the steps — flexible but order-dependent and drift-prone. The interview line: ‘I declare the destination; the tool figures out the route.’
How do you explain Terraform’s workflow? ||| Write declarative HCL, plan to preview the diff, apply to converge. State tracks reality (remote backend, locking!); modules compose reusable units. The interview traps: state drift, and why plan-before-apply is non-negotiable.
How do you discuss state management tradeoffs? ||| Lift state to the lowest common parent first; context for truly global low-churn data (theme, auth) — not as a state manager, since context re-renders all consumers; external stores (Zustand/Redux) for complex shared state with selective subscriptions.
How do you structure Terraform modules and composition? ||| Small composable modules (one concern each: network, compute, data), composed in environment roots with explicit inputs — no deep nesting. The interview line: ‘modules are functions; roots are the programs that call them.’
What is infrastructure drift, and how do you fight it? ||| Reality diverging from declared config (manual console clicks). Detection: scheduled plan runs that alert on diffs; prevention: make the console read-only, everything through code. The interview line: ‘drift is how outages hide.’

Q: The primary defense against drift is:

Symmetric vs asymmetric crypto — the interview version? ||| Symmetric (AES): one shared key, fast — for bulk data. Asymmetric (RSA/ECDH): key pairs, slow — for key exchange and signatures.
