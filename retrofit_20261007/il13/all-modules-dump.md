# swe-interview-foundations--dsa-big-o | Complexity Analysis
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: big-O, Big-Theta, Big-Omega; Understand and apply: time vs space: the two axes; Understand and apply: amortized analysis: dynamic arrays; Understand and apply: best, average, and worst-case reasoning; Understand and apply: log n from halving; n log n from divide-and-conquer
NOTE[What this covers]: - Big-O, Big-Theta, Big-Omega | - Time vs space: the two axes | - Amortized analysis: dynamic arrays | - Best, average, and worst-case reasoning | - Log n from halving; n log n from divide-and-conquer | - Talking through complexity in an interview
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: The vocabulary of every technical interview: reading a solution's cost at a glance and saying it out loud without hand-waving.
NOTE[Sources]: - **Python Documentation** (https://docs.python.org/3/) — The official language reference and tutorial. | - **LeetCode** (https://leetcode.com/) — The largest public interview-problem bank; the reps live here.
CARD: An interviewer asks: ‘What’s the time complexity of your solution?’ What does a strong answer include? || The Big-O bound, which case it describes (best/average/worst), the dominant operation driving it, and the space complexity — said out loud while pointing at the code, e.g. ‘O(n log n) time worst case from the sort, O(n) space for the output.’
CARD: Time vs space: how do you discuss the tradeoff? || As the two axes every solution is measured on: faster often costs memory (hash maps, memoization, caches), leaner often costs time (recompute vs store). The interview line: ‘I’ll name both complexities for every solution I propose.’
CARD: How do you explain amortized analysis of a dynamic array in an interview? || ‘Appending is O(1) amortized: most appends are constant time, and the occasional doubling resize costs O(n) but happens rarely enough that the cost spreads to O(1) per append across a sequence of n appends.’
CARD: Why do interviewers ask for best, average, AND worst case? || Because the bound alone hides behavior: quicksort is O(n log n) average but O(n²) worst case on sorted input — and the input distribution in the real system decides which one you actually pay.
CARD: How do you derive log n and n log n on the spot? || ‘Halving the problem each step gives log n levels — binary search. Divide-and-conquer that does linear work per level, like mergesort, gives n work × log n levels = n log n.’ Say the recurrence, then the level count.
QUIZ: Stating complexity aloud during coding: => [Makes your reasoning gradable and forces self-checks] Verbalized reasoning is scorable reasoning — and it catches your own mistakes mid-stream.
QUIZ: Binary search on n sorted elements is O(log n) because: => [Each comparison halves the remaining search space] Halving n times reaches 1 element: the number of halvings is log₂ n.
QUIZ: Quicksort with a naive first-element pivot on an already-sorted array degrades to: => [O(n²)] Bad pivots make partitions maximally unbalanced: n levels of n work each.
QUIZ: A dynamic array doubles its capacity when full. The amortized cost per append is: => [O(1)] The O(n) copy cost of a resize is spread over the ~n/2 appends since the last resize: O(1) each.

# swe-interview-foundations--dsa-patterns | Arrays, Strings, and Hashing Patterns
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: two pointers: same and opposite ends; Understand and apply: sliding windows: fixed and variable; Understand and apply: hash maps for O(1) lookups; Understand and apply: prefix sums and difference arrays; Understand and apply: monotonic stacks and queues
NOTE[What this covers]: - Two pointers: same and opposite ends | - Sliding windows: fixed and variable | - Hash maps for O(1) lookups | - Prefix sums and difference arrays | - Monotonic stacks and queues | - Pattern-recognition drills
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: The highest-yield module in the path: the handful of patterns covering the majority of array/string questions, trained until recognition is instant.
NOTE[Sources]: - **LeetCode** (https://leetcode.com/) — The largest public interview-problem bank; the reps live here. | - **NeetCode** (https://neetcode.io/) — Curated problem lists grouped by pattern; the pattern-first study order.
CARD: When do you reach for two pointers? || Ordered data and pair-finding: opposite ends for sorted two-sum or container-with-most-water; same-direction fast/slow pointers for cycle detection or in-place dedup — O(n) time, O(1) space. Name which variant and why.
CARD: Sliding window vs two pointers: what’s the distinction you’d state in an interview? || Sliding window is for contiguous subarrays/substrings under a constraint (max sum of k, longest substring without repeats): expand the right edge, shrink the left while the constraint is violated — O(n) because each element enters and leaves once.
CARD: Why is ‘use a hash map’ the most common interview unlock? || It trades space for time: O(1) average lookups turn O(n²) pair-searches into O(n) single passes — two-sum, frequency counts, memoization keys, and seen-sets all run on this trade.
CARD: What do prefix sums buy you in an interview? || O(1) range-sum queries after O(n) preprocessing: subarray sums become prefix[j] − prefix[i−1], and ‘subarray summing to k’ becomes a hash map over prefix values — the standard follow-up when brute force is O(n²).
CARD: When does a monotonic stack appear in interviews? || ‘Next greater element’ and histogram-rectangle problems: the stack keeps candidates in increasing order, and each element is pushed and popped once — O(n) for problems that look O(n²).
QUIZ: Pattern-recognition drilling works because interviews test: => [Mapping novel problems to known patterns under time pressure] You’ll never see the exact problem again — but you’ll see the pattern.
QUIZ: A monotonic stack solves ‘next greater element’ in O(n) because: => [Each element is pushed and popped at most once] Amortized O(1) work per element: once popped, an element never re-enters the stack.
QUIZ: With prefix[k] = sum of arr[0..k], the sum of arr[i..j] is: => [prefix[j] − prefix[i−1]] Subtract the sum before i from the sum through j; what remains is exactly arr[i..j].
QUIZ: A hash map reduces two-sum from O(n²) to O(n) because: => [Each complement lookup becomes O(1) average] For each x, checking whether target−x was seen is one hash lookup instead of a full rescan.

# swe-interview-foundations--dsa-trees-graphs | Trees, Graphs, and Recursion
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: recursion: base cases and the call stack; Understand and apply: binary trees: in-order, pre-order, post-order; Understand and apply: bSTs: invariants and operations; Understand and apply: heaps and priority queues; Understand and apply: graph representations: adjacency lists
NOTE[What this covers]: - Recursion: base cases and the call stack | - Binary trees: in-order, pre-order, post-order | - BSTs: invariants and operations | - Heaps and priority queues | - Graph representations: adjacency lists | - BFS, DFS, and when each fits
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: Non-linear structures without mystique: traversal fluency, the BST invariant, and the BFS/DFS decision reflex every interview expects.
NOTE[Sources]: - **Python Documentation** (https://docs.python.org/3/) — The official language reference and tutorial. | - **LeetCode** (https://leetcode.com/) — The largest public interview-problem bank; the reps live here.
CARD: How do you reason about recursion out loud in an interview? || State the base case first, then the recursive step’s contract: ‘assume it works for n−1, here’s how I build n.’ Then name the call-stack cost — depth × frame size — because interviewers probe stack depth and overflow.
CARD: In-order, pre-order, post-order — how do you keep them straight under pressure? || It’s about when you visit the node relative to its children: pre = before, in = between (gives sorted order on a BST), post = after (children before parent — natural for deletion and bottom-up computations).
CARD: What’s the BST invariant, and why do interviewers care? || ‘Left subtree < node < right subtree’ at every node. It buys O(log n) search/insert/delete when balanced — and the follow-up is always about what breaks it: degenerate insertion order gives O(n) without balancing.
CARD: Heap vs sorted array: how do you justify a heap in an interview? || When you need the min/max repeatedly but not full order: push/pop in O(log n), peek in O(1). Top-k, merge-k-sorted, and streaming medians are heap problems — say ‘I need repeated access to the extreme, not a full sort.’
CARD: How do you represent graphs in interviews? || Adjacency lists by default: O(V+E) memory, fast neighbor iteration — the right choice for sparse graphs (which interview graphs are). Adjacency matrix only for dense graphs or O(1) edge queries. The interview line: ‘list unless I can say why matrix.’
QUIZ: Shortest path in an unweighted graph is a BFS problem because: => [BFS explores in increasing distance from the source] BFS’s level order guarantees the first time you reach a node is via the fewest edges.
QUIZ: Adjacency lists beat matrices for typical interview graphs because: => [O(V+E) memory and fast neighbor iteration on sparse graphs] Interview graphs are sparse; matrices would waste O(V²) on empty edges.
QUIZ: Finding the k largest elements in a stream is a heap problem because: => [A min-heap of size k keeps the k largest with O(log k) updates] Keep a min-heap of the k best seen; each new element is compared against the heap top in O(log k).
QUIZ: A BST built by inserting the sorted keys 1..n degenerates to: => [A linked list with O(n) operations] Each insertion goes right every time; height becomes n and the log-n guarantee evaporates.

# swe-interview-foundations--dsa-dynamic | Dynamic Programming
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: recurrence first: overlapping subproblems; Understand and apply: memoization vs tabulation; Understand and apply: the 1D ladder: climbing stairs, house robber; Understand and apply: knapsack and subset patterns; Understand and apply: DP on strings: LCS and edit distance
NOTE[What this covers]: - Recurrence first: overlapping subproblems | - Memoization vs tabulation | - The 1D ladder: climbing stairs, house robber | - Knapsack and subset patterns | - DP on strings: LCS and edit distance | - State design: the interviewer's favorite question
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: DP demystified into a repeatable method: define the state, write the recurrence, then optimize — the module most candidates skip and most offers require.
NOTE[Sources]: - **LeetCode** (https://leetcode.com/) — The largest public interview-problem bank; the reps live here. | - **NeetCode** (https://neetcode.io/) — Curated problem lists grouped by pattern; the pattern-first study order.
CARD: What’s the first thing you write for a DP problem? || The recurrence in words, before any code: ‘dp[i] = …’ stated as a decision — e.g. ‘rob or skip house i.’ Interviewers score the recurrence; the code is transcription. Then name the overlapping subproblems that make memoization valid.
CARD: Memoization vs tabulation: how do you compare them in an interview? || Same complexity, different direction: memoization is top-down recursion + cache (natural to write, pays recursion overhead); tabulation is bottom-up iteration (no stack risk, often better constants). State both, pick one, justify.
CARD: What’s the 1D DP ladder? || The progression: climbing stairs (dp[i] = dp[i-1] + dp[i-2]) → house robber (add the skip-or-take decision) → harder 1D problems. Master the ladder and every 1D DP is a variation — the interview line: ‘I climb the ladder before I attempt the roof.’
CARD: How do you recognize a knapsack problem in disguise? || ‘Choose a subset under a capacity to maximize value’ — partition-equal-subset, coin change, and target-sum are all knapsack variants. Name the pattern out loud: ‘this is 0/1 knapsack on capacity W,’ then write the recurrence.
CARD: LCS and edit distance: what’s the shared DP skeleton? || A 2D table over the two strings’ prefixes: dp[i][j] from dp[i−1][j], dp[i][j−1], dp[i−1][j−1]. Match → extend the diagonal; mismatch → take the best neighbor. Say ‘prefixes table’ and the interviewer knows you see it.
QUIZ: Good DP state design means the state: => [Captures exactly the information needed to make future decisions — no more, no l] Too little state and the recurrence can’t decide; too much and you blow up the table for nothing.
QUIZ: In edit distance, dp[i][j] represents: => [The min edits to convert the first i chars of A to the first j chars of B] The prefixes-table framing: every cell solves the subproblem on the two prefixes.
QUIZ: The 0/1 knapsack decision at each item is: => [Take it or skip it, respecting remaining capacity] The 0/1 constraint forces a binary choice per item: dp[i][w] = max(skip, take).
QUIZ: House robber’s recurrence dp[i] = max(dp[i-1], dp[i-2] + nums[i]) encodes: => [The skip-or-take decision at each house] Each house: skip it (dp[i-1]) or rob it plus the best up to i-2.

# swe-interview-foundations--sysdesign-core | System Design Fundamentals
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: functional vs non-functional requirements; Understand and apply: back-of-envelope estimation; Understand and apply: load balancers, caches, CDNs; Understand and apply: SQL vs NoSQL: choosing honestly; Understand and apply: CAP theorem and consistency models
NOTE[What this covers]: - Functional vs non-functional requirements | - Back-of-envelope estimation | - Load balancers, caches, CDNs | - SQL vs NoSQL: choosing honestly | - CAP theorem and consistency models | - The standard interview walkthrough
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: The system-design interview as a skill: scoping, estimating, drawing the boxes, and defending tradeoffs — practiced, not memorized.
NOTE[Sources]: - **System Design Primer** (https://github.com/donnemartin/system-design-primer) — The open-source system-design study guide the industry passes around. | - **Redis Documentation** (https://redis.io/docs/) — Data structures, persistence, and clustering references.
CARD: How do you open a system design round? || Requirements before architecture: restate functional requirements (what the system does), then non-functional (scale, latency, availability, consistency). Scoping first proves you design the right system — candidates who draw boxes before asking get failed.
CARD: What are interviewers actually testing with back-of-envelope estimation? || Order-of-magnitude judgment, not arithmetic: powers of 10 for users, QPS, storage, and bandwidth, with stated assumptions. Round aggressively, narrate the assumptions, and drive toward a design decision (shard? cache? CDN?).
CARD: Load balancer, cache, CDN — the one-line job of each? || Load balancer: spread traffic across healthy servers. Cache: serve hot data without hitting the origin (Redis/Memcached). CDN: push static content to the edge near users. In a design, place them in that order along the request path.
CARD: SQL vs NoSQL: how do you ‘choose honestly’ in an interview? || Name the workload, not the hype: need joins, transactions, and flexible queries → relational; massive write scale, simple key access, flexible schema → NoSQL family by access pattern (document, key-value, wide-column). The honest answer always starts from the access patterns.
CARD: How do you discuss CAP without reciting the textbook? || ‘During a network partition, I choose between consistency and availability — and here’s which my system needs and why.’ Then name the real tradeoff in your design: e.g. ‘the feed can be eventually consistent, but payments cannot.’
QUIZ: CAP says a distributed system under partition must sacrifice: => [Either consistency or availability] Partitions are a fact of networks; the choice is CP (consistent, unavailable) or AP (available, inconsistent).
QUIZ: The deciding factor in SQL vs NoSQL should be: => [The data’s access patterns and consistency needs] Storage choice follows how data is read and written and what consistency the product needs.
QUIZ: A CDN primarily reduces: => [Latency for static content by serving from edge locations] Edge caches put bytes near users; origin round-trips disappear for cacheable content.
QUIZ: 100M daily active users making 10 requests each ≈ what average QPS? => [~12,000] 100M × 10 = 10⁹ requests/day ÷ 86,400 s/day ≈ 11,574 — roughly 12k QPS.

# backend-interview-prep--api-design-patterns | API Design Patterns
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: REST: resources, verbs, status codes; Understand and apply: idempotency and safe retries; Understand and apply: pagination, filtering, versioning; Understand and apply: graphQL: when queries beat endpoints; Understand and apply: gRPC and contract-first design
NOTE[What this covers]: - REST: resources, verbs, status codes | - Idempotency and safe retries | - Pagination, filtering, versioning | - GraphQL: when queries beat endpoints | - gRPC and contract-first design | - Error models clients can program against
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: APIs as the product: resource modeling, idempotent mutations, and error contracts — the design judgment backend interviews probe first.
NOTE[Sources]: - **RESTful API Design Guide** (https://restfulapi.net/) — Resource modeling, verbs, status codes, and versioning conventions. | - **martinfowler.com** (https://martinfowler.com/) — Long-form essays on API design, microservices, and architecture tradeoffs. | - **GraphQL Documentation** (https://graphql.org/learn/) — Schemas, queries, mutations, and the thinking behind them.
CARD: What makes an API ‘RESTful’ in an interview answer? || Resources as nouns in URLs, HTTP verbs as actions, status codes as the contract (200/201/400/401/403/404/429/500), stateless requests, and versioning — then the judgment calls: pagination, filtering, and idempotency.
CARD: How do you explain idempotency to an interviewer? || ‘Safe to retry’: repeating the request doesn’t change the outcome beyond the first application. Design it with idempotency keys on writes, and pair it with safe retries (exponential backoff + jitter) for distributed calls.
CARD: Offset vs cursor pagination: the interview-ready comparison? || Offset is simple but skips/duplicates rows when data changes and gets slower deep into the list; cursor (keyset) pagination is stable and O(1)-ish per page using an indexed column. Choose cursor for feeds, offset for admin UIs.
CARD: When do queries beat endpoints — your GraphQL judgment call? || When clients need varied, nested data and over/under-fetching hurts (mobile, aggregations): one query, exact fields, typed schema. The costs you must name: caching is harder, query complexity needs limits, and N+1 resolvers need batching (dataloaders).
CARD: When is gRPC the right answer? || Service-to-service inside the trust boundary: binary protobufs (fast, small), strict contracts via .proto, and streaming RPCs. The tradeoff: poor browser support and harder debugging than JSON — so internal microservices yes, public APIs usually no.
QUIZ: A good API error response includes a request ID so that: => [Support can correlate the client’s error with server-side logs] Correlation IDs connect the client’s report to the exact server-side trace.
QUIZ: gRPC’s contract-first design means: => [Services share .proto definitions so clients and servers stay in sync] The .proto file is the single source of truth generating typed clients and servers.
QUIZ: GraphQL’s main cost vs REST that you must name in an interview is: => [Caching and rate-limiting are harder without per-endpoint semantics] A single endpoint with arbitrary query shapes defeats HTTP caching and simple per-route limits — you need query-cost analysis.
QUIZ: Cursor pagination avoids the ‘missing rows’ problem of offset because: => [It pages relative to a stable key instead of a shifting row number] New inserts shift offsets; a keyset cursor anchors to the last seen key, immune to shifts.

# backend-interview-prep--data-layer | Databases and the Data Layer
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: schema design and normalization; Understand and apply: indexes: B-trees, covering, composite; Understand and apply: transactions and isolation levels; Understand and apply: noSQL families: document, key-value, wide-column; Understand and apply: connection pooling and N+1 queries
NOTE[What this covers]: - Schema design and normalization | - Indexes: B-trees, covering, composite | - Transactions and isolation levels | - NoSQL families: document, key-value, wide-column | - Connection pooling and N+1 queries | - Migrations without downtime
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: The data layer interviews actually test: index design, isolation levels, and the honest SQL-vs-NoSQL decision — not trivia, judgment.
NOTE[Sources]: - **PostgreSQL Documentation** (https://www.postgresql.org/docs/) — Indexes, transactions, isolation, and SQL done right. | - **MongoDB Documentation** (https://www.mongodb.com/docs/) — Document modeling, aggregation, and indexing. | - **Redis Documentation** (https://redis.io/docs/) — Data structures, persistence, and clustering references.
CARD: How do you discuss schema design and normalization? || Normalize to remove redundancy (1NF→2NF→3NF: atomic values, no partial or transitive dependencies), then denormalize deliberately where read performance demands it — documenting why. The interview line: ‘normalize by default, denormalize by measurement.’
CARD: How do you explain indexes in a backend interview? || B-tree indexes turn O(n) scans into O(log n) lookups on the indexed columns — at the cost of write overhead and storage. Composite indexes serve multi-column filters left-to-right; covering indexes answer the query without touching the table.
CARD: How do you talk about transaction isolation levels? || The ladder: read uncommitted → read committed → repeatable read → serializable, trading anomalies (dirty reads, non-repeatable reads, phantoms) for concurrency. Name the anomaly your workload can’t tolerate, then pick the level.
CARD: Name the NoSQL families and their fit in one breath. || Document (flexible JSON, e.g. product catalogs), key-value (blazing simple lookups, sessions/caches), wide-column (massive write scale, time series), graph (relationship-heavy traversals). Fit follows access pattern.
CARD: What’s the N+1 query problem and its fix? || Loading N parents then one query per parent’s children: N+1 round trips. The fix is eager loading — a JOIN or batched second query (and ORMs have explicit eager-loading APIs). Name it when you see loops over queries.
QUIZ: The expand-contract migration pattern’s core idea is: => [Backward-compatible steps where old and new code coexist] Every intermediate state must work with both old and new code; the old shape is removed only after readers move.
QUIZ: Eager loading fixes N+1 by: => [Fetching related rows in bulk (JOIN or batched query) instead of one query per p] One bulk fetch replaces N per-row queries; round trips collapse from N+1 to ~2.
QUIZ: A shopping-cart session store is a natural key-value fit because: => [Access is by a single session key with simple read/write] Single-key access with no relations is exactly the key-value sweet spot.
QUIZ: Repeatable read prevents ___ but still allows ___: => [Non-repeatable reads; phantoms] Repeatable read holds read locks so re-reads are stable, but new rows matching the predicate (phantoms) can still appear.

# backend-interview-prep--caching-queues | Caching and Message Queues
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: cache-aside, read-through, write-through; Understand and apply: TTL, invalidation, and the thundering herd; Understand and apply: redis data structures in practice; Understand and apply: queues: at-least-once vs exactly-once; Understand and apply: kafka topics, partitions, consumer groups
NOTE[What this covers]: - Cache-aside, read-through, write-through | - TTL, invalidation, and the thundering herd | - Redis data structures in practice | - Queues: at-least-once vs exactly-once | - Kafka topics, partitions, consumer groups | - Backpressure and dead-letter queues
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: Scale's two workhorses: caches that absorb reads and queues that absorb bursts — with the failure modes (stampedes, poison messages) interviewers love to ask about.
NOTE[Sources]: - **Redis Documentation** (https://redis.io/docs/) — Data structures, persistence, and clustering references. | - **Apache Kafka Documentation** (https://kafka.apache.org/documentation/) — Topics, partitions, consumer groups, and delivery semantics.
CARD: Cache-aside vs read-through vs write-through? || Cache-aside: app checks cache, falls back to DB, populates cache (flexible, stale-risk). Read-through: cache loads from DB itself. Write-through: writes go to cache and DB together (consistent, slower writes). Name the invalidation story for whichever you pick.
CARD: What’s the thundering herd, and how do you stop it? || A hot cache key expires and every request stampedes the DB at once. Fixes: request coalescing (single-flight — one request loads, others wait), probabilistic early refresh, or staggered TTLs with jitter.
CARD: Why does Redis show up in backend interviews constantly? || Because it’s five tools in one: strings (counters, rate limits), hashes (objects), lists (queues), sets (unique membership), sorted sets (leaderboards, time-ordered feeds) — all in-memory with persistence options. Name the structure that fits the access pattern.

CARD: Threads vs processes vs event loops? || Processes: isolated memory, expensive. Threads: shared memory, cheap-ish, need locks. Event loop: single-threaded async I/O (Node, asyncio) — great for I/O-bound, terrible for CPU-bound. Match the model to the bottleneck.

QUIZ: An API that should allow brief traffic spikes should use: => [Token bucket] Accumulated tokens absorb bursts up to bucket capacity; a leaky bucket would queue or drop them.

# embedded-swe-interview-prep--c-memory | C and Memory Models

NOTE[Sources]: - **cppreference** (https://en.cppreference.com/) — The C (and C++) language reference interviewers trust. | - **GCC Online Documentation** (https://gcc.gnu.org/onlinedocs/) — Compiler behavior, extensions, and C dialect details.
CARD: What’s array-pointer decay, and why do interviewers probe it? || Arrays decay to pointers to their first element in most expressions — sizeof stops working across function boundaries. The interview line: ‘inside the function it’s a pointer; always pass the length explicitly.’
CARD: Stack vs heap vs static in embedded? || Stack: automatic, fast, tiny — overflow is the classic crash. Heap: often banned (fragmentation, nondeterminism) in RTOS firmware. Static: fixed at link time, deterministic — the embedded default. The interview line: ‘I can tell you my worst-case RAM usage at compile time.’
CARD: What does ‘volatile’ actually do, and when is it required? || It tells the compiler the value can change outside program flow (hardware registers, ISR-shared flags) — so no caching in registers, no reordering/eliding reads. Required for memory-mapped registers and ISR-shared flags; it is NOT a substitute for atomics or barriers.
CARD: Why do structs have padding, and when do you pack them? || Alignment: CPUs require (or prefer) multi-byte types at aligned addresses — the compiler inserts padding. CARD: How do you discuss undefined behavior in a C interview? || As the compiler’s license to assume: signed overflow, null dereference, use-after-free, data races — ‘anything can happen’ includes ‘works until the optimizer upgrades.’ The interview line: ‘I write so UB can’t happen; sanitizers prove it.’
QUIZ: Fluency in reading C matters for embedded interviews because: => [Datasheets, HALs, and legacy drivers are C — you inherit them before you rewrite] You’ll read ten times more C than you write; reading speed is the bottleneck.
QUIZ: Signed integer overflow in C is: => [Undefined behavior — the optimizer may assume it never happens] Compilers exploit the UB assumption for optimization; ‘works in debug’ proves nothing.
QUIZ: A packed struct is required when: => [The memory layout must match an exact wire/disk format with no padding] Protocols define byte-exact layouts; compiler padding would corrupt the format.
QUIZ: A status register polled in a loop must be volatile because: => [The compiler would otherwise optimize the repeated read away, never seeing hardw] Without volatile, the compiler may read once and reuse the cached value forever.

CARD: How do you explain RTOS scheduling? || Priority-based preemptive: the highest-priority ready task runs, preemption on events/ticks, deterministic context switches. Contrast with GPOS time-slicing fairness — an RTOS optimizes for deadline guarantees, not throughput.
CARD: What’s the cost of preemption and context switches? || Saving/restoring registers, stack switch, cache/TLB disturbance — microseconds that add up at high tick rates. The interview line: ‘context switches are the RTOS’s tax; I budget them like any other cost.’
CARD: What’s tickless idle, and why does power care? || Instead of waking on every OS tick, the RTOS programs the next wakeup (timer or interrupt) and sleeps deeply until then — no periodic wakeups burning power. The interview line: ‘every avoided wakeup is battery life.’

QUIZ: Zephyr’s device-tree-driven configuration means: => [Hardware description lives in data files, not #ifdefs — portable across boards] Board differences become data, not code branches — the same firmware builds for many targets.
QUIZ: Object pools replace malloc in RTOS firmware because: => [Fixed-size, deterministic, fragmentation-free — bounded worst-case behavior] Pools give dynamic lifetimes with static guarantees: the embedded compromise.
QUIZ: Tickless idle saves power by: => [Eliminating periodic tick wakeups — sleeping until the next real event] Deep sleep between events beats waking a thousand times a second to do nothing.
QUIZ: High tick rates hurt because: => [Each tick can force context-switch overhead — the tax compounds with frequency] Tickless designs exist precisely to avoid paying this tax when there’s no work.

# embedded-swe-interview-prep--embedded-concurrency | Concurrency and Interrupts
OBJECTIVES: Understand and apply: iSRs: short, no blocking; Understand and apply: critical sections and disabling interrupts; Understand and apply: mutexes vs semaphores in an RTOS; Understand and apply: priority inversion and inheritance; Understand and apply: race conditions at the register level
NOTE[What this covers]: - ISRs: short, no blocking | - Critical sections and disabling interrupts | - Mutexes vs semaphores in an RTOS | - Priority inversion and inheritance | - Race conditions at the register level | - Lock-free ring buffers
NOTE[How Interstitium Labs teaches this]: Concurrency where the stakes are hardware: ISRs, priority inversion, and the synchronization patterns that keep real-time systems correct.
NOTE[Sources]: - **FreeRTOS Documentation** (https://www.freertos.org/) — Tasks, queues, semaphores: the RTOS most interviews assume. | - **Linux man pages** (https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/) — System calls and C library functions, authoritatively.
CARD: What are the ISR rules you’d state in an interview? || Short, no blocking, no heap, minimal work — set a flag or push to a queue and let a task do the heavy lifting. Protect shared data (critical sections), and know your interrupt priorities vs the RTOS kernel’s.
CARD: How do you protect a critical section on a microcontroller? || Briefly disable interrupts (or raise to a priority mask) around the shared access — shortest possible region. For task-task sharing, RTOS mutexes; for ISR-task sharing, interrupt masking is the tool. The interview line: ‘critical sections are measured in instructions, not functions.’
CARD: Mutex vs semaphore in an RTOS? || Mutex: ownership + priority inheritance — for mutual exclusion. Semaphore: counting, no ownership — for signaling and resource pools. The interview line: ‘I never use a semaphore where I need ownership semantics.’
CARD: Explain priority inversion and its fix. || A low-priority task holds a mutex a high-priority task needs; a medium-priority task preempts the low one — the high task waits unboundedly. CARD: What does a race condition look like at the register level? || Read-modify-write on a shared peripheral register: ISR fires between the read and the write, clobbering the ISR’s change. Fixes: atomic set/clear registers (hardware), critical sections, or the lock-free ring buffer for data handoff.
QUIZ: A single-producer/single-consumer ring buffer avoids locks because: => [Producer and consumer each own one index — no shared mutable state to race on] Head owned by consumer, tail by producer: disjoint writes need no mutual exclusion.
QUIZ: Read-modify-write races happen because: => [The operation isn’t atomic — an interrupt between read and write loses the inter] Two writers interleaved across a non-atomic sequence: the last write wins and one update vanishes.
QUIZ: Priority inheritance fixes inversion by: => [Temporarily boosting the lock holder to the blocked high-priority task’s level] The holder can’t be preempted by medium-priority tasks anymore, so it releases the lock promptly.
QUIZ: A mutex’s ownership (vs a binary semaphore) enables: => [Priority inheritance and unlock-by-owner checks — the RTOS knows who holds it] Ownership is what lets the kernel boost the holder’s priority and catch misuse.

CARD: Draw the test pyramid in words. || Many fast unit tests at the base, fewer integration tests in the middle, few end-to-end tests at the top. The economics: e2e is slow, flaky, and expensive — push coverage down the pyramid, and justify every e2e test’s existence.
CARD: What belongs at each test layer? || Unit: pure logic and algorithms. Integration: component interactions (DB, API contracts). E2E: critical user journeys only. The interview line: ‘each layer tests what only that layer can prove.’
CARD: Mocks vs stubs vs fakes? || Dummy: placeholder. Stub: canned responses. Fake: working simplified implementation (in-memory DB). Mock: verifies interactions (was this called?). Use fakes/stubs for state, mocks sparingly for interaction contracts.
CARD: What do risk-based planning and shift-left mean in practice? || Risk-based: test effort follows failure probability × impact — not uniform coverage. Shift-left: move testing earlier (CI gates, pre-merge checks) so defects are found when they’re cheapest. The interview line: ‘I spend test budget where failure hurts most.’
CARD: How do you argue test strategy in an interview? || From risk: what’s most likely to break × what hurts most — then the pyramid allocation that covers it, with the economics stated. The interview line: ‘strategy is resource allocation under uncertainty, argued out loud.’
QUIZ: Leading with risk (not coverage %) is strong because: => [It shows you allocate testing where failure actually hurts — engineering judgmen] 100% coverage of the wrong things is worse than 60% of the right things.
QUIZ: Shift-left reduces cost because: => [Defects found earlier are dramatically cheaper to fix than escaped ones] The cost-of-defect curve rises steeply with lateness; early gates flatten it.
QUIZ: A mock differs from a stub in that it: => [Verifies interactions (calls made), while a stub just returns canned data] Stubs provide state; mocks assert behavior — over-mocking couples tests to implementation.
QUIZ: An E2E test for input validation is usually wrong because: => [Validation logic is provable in milliseconds at the unit layer — E2E is the most] Push each check to the cheapest layer that can prove it.

CARD: How do you explain Selenium WebDriver’s architecture? || Language bindings → WebDriver protocol (HTTP/JSON wire) → browser driver → real browser. That layering is why it’s cross-browser and cross-language — and why it needs explicit waits: the protocol has no built-in actionability checks.
CARD: Why does Playwright win framework comparisons lately? || Auto-waiting (actions wait for actionability — no sleep/flaky waits), multiple browser engines, network interception, parallel execution, and first-class async. CARD: What’s the Page Object Model buying you? || One class per page encapsulating selectors and actions: tests read as user intent (‘loginAs(user)’), and UI changes touch one place. The interview line: ‘selectors live in exactly one file per screen.’
CARD: What are Cypress’s tradeoffs? || Runs in-browser: great debugging (time travel, real DOM access), automatic waiting — but same-run origin limits (iframes/multi-domain need workarounds) and no multi-tab. The interview line: ‘Cypress trades browser-architecture limits for the best debugging experience.’
CARD: Appium basics — how do you position it? || WebDriver-protocol mobile automation: one API driving iOS (XCUITest) and Android (UiAutomator2) — cross-platform scripts at the cost of speed and flakiness vs native frameworks. The interview line: ‘Appium for cross-platform coverage; native frameworks where depth matters.’
QUIZ: Selection criteria must come before candidate tools because: => [Criteria-first prevents rationalizing a pre-chosen favorite] Tools-first selection is advocacy; criteria-first is engineering.
QUIZ: Appium’s core value proposition is: => [One WebDriver-style API automating both iOS and Android] Cross-platform mobile tests through a unified protocol — trading some speed for coverage breadth.
QUIZ: Cypress’s architectural tradeoff is: => [In-browser execution gives superb debugging but same-origin/tab limitations] Running inside the browser enables time-travel debugging; the cost is the browser’s own sandbox limits.
QUIZ: The Page Object Model’s main benefit is: => [Encapsulating page structure so UI changes don’t rewrite every test] A selector change updates one page object instead of dozens of tests.

CARD: Load vs stress vs spike vs soak? || Load: expected traffic, measure latency/throughput. Stress: beyond capacity, find the breaking point. Spike: sudden surges, test autoscaling. Soak: sustained load over hours, find leaks. Report percentiles (p95/p99), not averages — averages hide the tail.
CARD: Why do performance interviews insist on p95/p99? || Averages hide the tail: a 100ms average with a 5s p99 means 1% of users suffer. SLOs are written on percentiles because user experience lives in the tail. Always report the distribution, never just the mean.
CARD: k6 vs JMeter — how do you compare them? || k6: JS-scripted, developer-friendly, great for CI-integrated load tests with thresholds as gates. JMeter: GUI-driven, mature, huge protocol support, heavier. The interview line: ‘k6 for code-reviewed load tests in the pipeline; JMeter where the team already lives in it.’
CARD: What are performance-adjacent API checks, and how do you read a load-test report? || Adjacent checks: response-time budgets per endpoint in CI, payload sizes, N+1 detection. Reading a report: percentiles (not averages), error rate vs load steps, saturation signals, and the bottleneck’s location. The interview line: ‘I read the tail, then find what’s saturated.’
CARD: How do you discuss performance in an interview? || With numbers and method: the budget, the measurement (percentiles), the bottleneck found, the fix, the re-measurement. The interview line: ‘performance claims without before/after numbers are stories.’
QUIZ: Before/after numbers matter because: => [They prove the fix worked and quantify the win — otherwise it’s anecdote] Optimization without measurement is indistinguishable from guessing.
QUIZ: A load-test report’s most important numbers are: => [Percentile latencies and error rates per load step — the tail under stress] Averages hide the user experience; percentiles per step reveal where it breaks.
QUIZ: k6’s thresholds turn a load test into: => [A gate: breaching p95/error-rate assertions fails the run] Thresholds are pass/fail assertions on metrics — the test proves the SLO or it fails.
QUIZ: p99 latency of 2s means: => [99% of requests were faster than 2s — 1% were slower] Percentiles describe the distribution’s tail: p99 is the near-worst case users feel.

QUIZ: Defect escape rate measures: => [Bugs found in production that testing missed — the headline quality metric] It answers the only question stakeholders care about: did quality gates work?
QUIZ: Severity vs priority differ in that: => [Severity = impact on users; priority = order of fixing (business decision)] A severe bug in a deprecated feature can be low priority; a trivial bug blocking launch is high priority.
