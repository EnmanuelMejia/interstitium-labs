Ops math — role loop

Own the quantitative loop: measure → model → compute → verify → decide. Operations is applied arithmetic wearing a trench coat — availability targets, latency budgets, subnet plans, and capacity forecasts are all math problems first. The engineer who can show the work wins the whiteboard.

  1. Measure the system: collect the numbers that describe reality, not vibes.
  2. Model the question: turn "is this fast enough?" into an equation.
  3. Compute carefully: units, orders of magnitude, and the arithmetic itself.
  4. Verify by estimation: sanity-check every result before trusting it.
  5. Decide and defend: present the numbers and the reasoning behind them.

Four-phase timeline

Self-paced and adaptive: placement finds your ready-to-learn fringe, and spaced revisits keep rust off mastered topics. Proof over watch-time — every phase ends with worked math you can defend.

Phase 1 · Foundations

  • Arithmetic and units refresh. Rates, ratios, and unit conversion done cleanly. Why it matters: capacity and latency are unit problems — most ops math errors are unit errors.
  • Estimation and order of magnitude. Fermi-style reasoning for back-of-envelope checks. Why it matters: the fastest way to catch a wrong dashboard is to know roughly what right looks like.
  • Fractions, decimals, and percentages fluency. Convert and compare without hesitation. Why it matters: error budgets, availability, and growth rates are all percentages — fluency is speed.

Phase 2 · Core

  • The "nines." Availability math: what 99.9% actually allows per month, and how nines compound. Why it matters: SLO conversations are math conversations — this is the vocabulary.
  • Latency statistics. Mean vs. median vs. percentiles, and why averages lie. Why it matters: tail latency is user experience; p99 thinking separates operators from guessers.
  • Error budget arithmetic. From SLO to allowable failure, and how budgets get spent. Why it matters: turns reliability policy into numbers you can put in a runbook.
  • Data aggregation math. Sums, averages, and rates over grouped data. Why it matters: ops reporting — cost reviews, capacity asks, incident stats — is aggregation work.

Phase 3 · Advanced

  • CIDR and subnetting. Binary, masks, and address planning. Why it matters: every VPC and network design is arithmetic — interviewers still whiteboard this.
  • Capacity planning math. Growth rates, headroom, and queueing intuition. Why it matters: scaling decisions are forecasts; the math keeps them honest.
  • Probability basics for ops. Independent events, expected value, and risk framing. Why it matters: "how likely is a correlated failure?" is a probability question wearing an ops costume.

Phase 4 · Proof

  • Whiteboard SLO defense. Derive an error budget live, narrating every step. Why it matters: interviews test reasoning, not calculators — show the work out loud.
  • VPC design write-up. A worked CIDR plan with the binary shown. Why it matters: a design doc with visible math is portfolio evidence, not a claim.
  • Spaced mastery. Revisit decayed topics on a schedule. Why it matters: math rusts — the adaptive loop keeps the fringe clear.

Capstone: Ops Math Portfolio

Produce three worked write-ups, every step shown:

  1. SLO and error-budget analysis. Pick a service, derive its budget from the SLO, and write the spend policy — with the arithmetic visible.
  2. CIDR design document. Plan an address space for a multi-tier deployment, binary work shown, with headroom justified.
  3. Capacity plan. From current usage and a growth rate, forecast the scaling point and the headroom decision — estimation checks included.

Graduate claim: not "I'm good at math," but "here are three quantitative analyses I produced, and I can walk you through any line."

FAQ

  • What are the prerequisites?
    Basic arithmetic. Phase 1 is a true refresh — if you can add, multiply, and convert units, you can start.
  • How long does it take?
    Self-paced. The adaptive loop compresses what you already know and expands what you don't — your fringe sets the pace, not a syllabus.
  • Is this affiliated with a leading adaptive-learning platform?
    No. The path borrows the adaptive mastery-learning tradition — placement, ready-to-learn fringe, spaced revisits — but it is independent Interstitium material with no affiliation to a leading adaptive-learning platform, McGraw Hill, or any assessment vendor, and it is not a placement exam.
  • Does completing the path grant a certificate?
    No. You leave with a portfolio of worked analyses — the kind of evidence that actually moves an SRE or DevOps interview.
  • How does this connect to the SRE path?
    Directly: the SRE path assumes SLO math, percentile thinking, and CIDR fluency. Ops Math is the quantitative foundation the SRE interview spine stands on — take it first or alongside.
