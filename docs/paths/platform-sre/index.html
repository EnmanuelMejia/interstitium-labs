Platform SRE — role loop

Own the reliability loop: define → instrument → defend the budget → automate → respond → learn. SRE is not "ops with a pager" and not "DevOps with a new title" — it is engineering applied to operations problems, where every decision is priced against a reliability budget.

  1. Define what "healthy" means in user terms — pick service-level indicators before shopping for tools.
  2. Instrument the system so the indicators are measurable, then set objectives and price the error budget.
  3. Defend the budget: spend it on velocity or reliability deliberately, never by accident.
  4. Automate the toil away — if a human does it twice, a machine should do it the third time.
  5. Respond to incidents with command discipline, then convert the pain into prevention.
  6. Learn in the open: blameless postmortems, runbooks, and narratives you can defend in an interview.

Four-phase timeline

Self-paced. Each phase ends with portfolio evidence, not a quiz score — proof over watch-time.

Phase 1 · Foundations

  • Linux systems literacy. Processes, signals, filesystems, and networking primitives. Why it matters: every reliability decision bottoms out at the operating system — you cannot debug what you cannot model.
  • Git and configuration discipline. Branching, history hygiene, change review. Why it matters: unreviewed change is the leading cause of incidents; the version history is your first observability tool.
  • Observability literacy. Logs, metrics, and traces — what each answers and what it costs. Why it matters: you cannot defend a budget you cannot measure.

Phase 2 · Core

  • SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets. Choose indicators users feel, set objectives, and price reliability as a budget. Why it matters: turns "make it reliable" from a slogan into a trade decision you can explain to a product manager.
  • Toil inventory. Measure manual operations work, classify it, and eliminate the top item. Why it matters: toil is the tax that stops SRE from being engineering — interviewers ask what you automated.
  • Alerting that respects humans. Page on symptoms, link every alert to a runbook, tune for signal. Why it matters: alert fatigue destroys on-call trust faster than any outage.
  • Incident basics. Timeline discipline, roles, and status communication. Why it matters: how you behave during the incident is half the interview.

Phase 3 · Advanced

  • GitOps and progressive delivery. Declarative desired state, canary rollouts, practiced rollbacks. Why it matters: deploys are the most common incident source — the pipeline is a reliability control.
  • Policy as code. Admission gates, drift detection, explainable constraints. Why it matters: prevention beats detection for entire classes of misconfiguration.
  • Kubernetes platform literacy. Workloads, controllers, and networking as the substrate your SLOs live on. Why it matters: modern SRE conversations assume the platform — fluency here is table stakes.

Phase 4 · Proof

  • Incident command drills. Run a simulated outage with roles, comms, and a timeline. Why it matters: command presence under pressure is demonstrated, not claimed.
  • STAR incident narratives. Convert two drills into portfolio-ready write-ups. Why it matters: hiring managers remember stories; artifacts make them credible.
  • Reliability review. Present your capstone's SLOs, budget policy, and incident learnings as a defense. Why it matters: the capstone is rehearsal for the architecture round.

Assessments

Assessment here is readiness-gated, not grade-gated. You advance by demonstrating the skill, not by passing a multiple-choice gate:

  • Adaptive placement on Adapt finds your ready-to-learn fringe so you start where the gaps are, not at page one.
  • Timed drills in Prep drills — including the desk interview track — rehearse SLO whiteboarding, toil-elimination pitches, and incident STAR narratives under interview conditions.
  • Explain-back checks: defend one SLO, one policy gate, and one postmortem out loud. If you cannot teach it, you have not mastered it.

No reproduced exam questions appear anywhere on this path. Practice is original; the vendor's exam belongs to the vendor.

Capstone: Reliable Checkout

Take a demo service on the SuperLab and make it demonstrably reliable:

  1. Write two user-facing SLOs and an error budget policy with explicit spend rules.
  2. Ship the service through a GitOps pipeline with progressive delivery and a practiced rollback.
  3. Add a policy-as-code gate that blocks a known-bad configuration class.
  4. Run a controlled incident drill: timeline, roles, status comms, and a blameless postmortem.
  5. Deliver the package: SLO one-pager, budget policy, runbook, postmortem, and a ten-minute reliability review you can give in an interview.

Graduate claim: not "I watched the videos," but "here is a service I made reliable, with the numbers and the paper trail to prove it."

FAQ

  • What are the prerequisites?
    Comfort at a Linux terminal and basic Git. No prior on-call experience — the incident drills teach the command discipline from zero.
  • How long does it take?
    Self-paced. Move as fast as your evidence allows — phases unlock on demonstrated skill, not calendar weeks.
  • How does this relate to vendor certifications?
    Honestly: this path builds the SRE concepts vendors test — reliability engineering, Kubernetes, infrastructure as code — but it is preparation, not the exam. No affiliation is claimed, no exam content is reproduced, and this path confers no certificate. When you are ready, certify directly with the vendor.
  • Does completing the path grant a certificate?
    No. You leave with portfolio artifacts — SLO one-pagers, runbooks, postmortems, and a capstone review — which carry more weight in hiring than a badge from an independent study site.
