Role loop

Think like the admin who gets paged: observe → diagnose → fix → harden → document. Every phase trains that loop until it is reflex: read the system, find the fault, repair it, make it harder to break, and leave a runbook behind.

Gamified mastery modules — four phases

Sixteen modules in four gated phases — what you learn, and why it matters on call or in an interview. Each phase ends with a gate: quiz, hands-on lab, timed sim, and oral defense.

  1. Phase 1

    Foundations — the shell is the job

    • Shell fluency

      Pipes, redirection, grep, sed, and awk basics. Why it matters: the shell is the sysadmin's IDE — everything else is built on it.

    • Users, groups & permissions

      Ownership, modes, ACLs, umask, and the sticky bit. Why it matters: permission errors are the number-one time sink on shared systems.

    • Text processing & editing

      Survival-level vim, log triage, and structured searching. Why it matters: you will read more logs than code for the rest of your career.

    • Package management

      dnf/rpm, repositories, and rollbacks. Why it matters: installing software correctly — and reversibly — is day-one work.

  2. Phase 2

    Core — services, storage, network

    • systemd services

      Units, targets, dependencies, and journalctl. Why it matters: everything on a modern Linux box is a unit, and failed units are the first place on-call looks.

    • Storage

      Partitions, LVM, filesystems, and mounting. Why it matters: disks fill and fail; extending a volume without downtime is bread-and-butter admin work.

    • Networking

      Addressing, routes, nmcli, and the DNS resolution path. Why it matters: “can't reach the host” starts here, and CIDR math shows up in every SRE screen.

    • firewalld & SELinux basics

      Zones, services, ports, and SELinux booleans. Why it matters: these are the layers people disable instead of understanding — don't be those people.

  3. Phase 3

    Advanced — the senior answers

    • SELinux in depth

      Contexts, policies, and troubleshooting denials with audit logs. Why it matters: this is the single biggest separator between junior and senior RHEL answers.

    • NFS & autofs

      Exports, mounts, and automounted home directories. Why it matters: enterprise file services still run on this, and it breaks in subtle ways.

    • Containers on Linux

      Podman, rootless containers, and systemd integration. Why it matters: modern RHEL runs containers natively — no daemon required.

    • Performance

      top, vmstat, iostat, and sar literacy plus basic tuning. Why it matters: “the server is slow” needs evidence, not guesses.

  4. Phase 4

    Proof — earn the spine

    • Timed troubleshooting

      Broken boot, full disk, failed service, unreachable host — diagnosed against the clock. Why it matters: pressure rewards method, and method is trainable.

    • Bash automation

      Scripting the repetitive admin tasks from earlier phases. Why it matters: admins who script scale themselves; the rest stay ticket-bound.

    • Hardening

      SSH lockdown, user hygiene, update policy, and basic auditing. Why it matters: a secure baseline is a deliverable skill, not a vibe.

    • Evidence runbooks

      Written runbooks for every major failure you fixed. Why it matters: interviews want stories with artifacts, and runbooks are both.

Assessments

Here, assessment is evidence, not watch-time. Each phase ends with a gate: a short adaptive quiz that finds your weak spots, a SuperLab task lab graded on outcome (did the thing actually work?), and a timed troubleshooting sim where you diagnose a broken system against the clock. Missed concepts remediate into /adapt/ fringe sessions instead of failing you outright. Timed Linux sims live in the drill library. Oral defense matters too: after every phase you explain a past failure out loud in STAR format, because interviews are oral exams.

Capstone

Capstone: “On-call Friday.” You are handed a VM in a bad state: it barely boots, /var is full, the firewall blocks the monitoring agent, and an SELinux denial is silently killing the web app. Restore full service, document the recovery order, then harden the box (SSH, users, updates) and ship a runbook another admin could follow at 3 AM. Graduate with the runbook, the before/after configs, and a STAR story for each failure.

FAQ

What should I know before starting?

Basic comfort at a terminal — navigating directories, editing files. Everything else is taught from the ground up.

How long does it take?

Self-paced. The four phases are sequential and gated; move at the speed your schedule allows, not a fixed calendar.

Is this the RHCSA exam?

No. This is independent preparation built by Interstitium Labs — not affiliated with or endorsed by Red Hat, and it confers no certificate. It builds the hands-on skills the credential tests, so you can earn it elsewhere with confidence.

Are these real exam questions?

No. All quizzes, drills, and sims are original practice material. We never reproduce exam questions.

What do I need to run the labs?

A Linux VM or container host you can break safely — local virtualization or SuperLab both work.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

  • What is the “spine”?
    The Linux core every other path assumes: shell, users and permissions, processes and systemd, disks and LVM, networking and firewall, containers on Linux. Earn the spine and the rest of the catalog gets easier.
  • Why is LVM and fsck a “story”?
    Because disk failures are narratives, not commands: something broke, you diagnose, you repair, you verify. The phase teaches the story shape so you can tell it in an interview and live it on call.
  • Why are containers in a Linux path?
    Because containers are Linux primitives — namespaces, cgroups, capabilities. Understanding the substrate makes container behavior predictable instead of magical.
  • Does this grant the RHCSA?
    No. Red Hat confers the RHCSA; this is the readiness spine — shell citizenship through senior-level answers, with lab evidence. The peer spine (Red Hat Learning, LF Linux) is named as the benchmark.
  • What is the “senior answers” advanced phase?
    The why behind the commands: tradeoffs, failure modes, and the judgment calls that separate a technician from an engineer. Same tools, deeper reasoning.
  • How is the spine assessed?
    By operating: services fixed, storage recovered, networks debugged. “Proof — earn the spine” means demonstration, and the artifacts are yours to keep.
