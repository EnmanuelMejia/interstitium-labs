The track

Money is a state machine. Learn to build it.

A blockchain is a replicated state machine with an adversarial threat model: a network of mutually suspicious computers that must agree, without a referee, on one ordered history of who owns what. Strip away the speculation and the branding, and every ledger reduces to the same engineering questions — how is state represented, who gets to write, how do strangers agree, and what happens when someone lies? Bitcoin answered them with unspent transaction outputs and proof-of-work; Ethereum with accounts and a global virtual machine; the XRP Ledger with federated consensus and three-second ledgers; Monero with cryptography that hides the transaction itself; Stellar with trust you configure yourself.

This track is the full stack of that engineering. It opens with the two canonical ledger architectures — Bitcoin's UTXO model and Ethereum's account model — taught down to the transaction bytes, then goes deep on smart-contract security the way auditors practice it: reentrancy, oracle manipulation, fuzzing, audit reports, deployment hygiene. It then widens to the payments layer where the user of this track does his real work: the XRP Ledger's consensus protocol and native DEX, ISO 20022's banking messaging standard and what it actually means when a coin claims to intersect it, Monero's privacy stack, and Stellar's federated agreement and anchors. The closing survey maps the ledger technology behind the major coins into protocol families — proof-of-work variants, proof-of-stake variants, BFT interoperability chains, DAG designs, oracle networks — with an honest accounting of each family's trade-offs, and finishes with custody engineering and a capstone where you select and defend a protocol for a real product.

Every module below explains its concepts in prose — no empty outlines — and every hands-on lab runs on test networks, regtest, or local emulators. No real funds. Finish the track and you can read a raw Bitcoin transaction by hand, threat-model a smart contract, reason about why a payment rail chose the XRP Ledger over Ethereum, and tell a colleague, precisely, what ISO 20022 standardizes and what it does not.

The machinery

The ideas, stated plainly.

The ledger as a replicated state machine

Think of a ledger not as "a blockchain" but as a state machine replicated across machines that do not trust each other: there is a current state (who owns what), a set of allowed transitions (valid transactions), and a mechanism for all replicas to apply the same transitions in the same order. "The chain" is just the log of transitions. Once you see it this way, every design decision in this track becomes a question about state representation, transition rules, or ordering.

UTXO vs. account models

Bitcoin represents money as discrete unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs) — each one a locked coin with a puzzle attached; spending a coin means unlocking it and relocking the pieces elsewhere. There are no accounts at the protocol layer, only outputs waiting to be consumed. Ethereum instead keeps accounts with balances, like bank ledgers, and transactions mutate balances directly. The UTXO model makes validation local and parallel (each output is independent); the account model makes shared mutable state natural (which is why smart contracts thrive there) but forces every transaction to contend over the same global state. Most ledgers are one of these two, or a hybrid — Cardano's eUTxO attaches contract data to outputs to get programmability without accounts.

Consensus: ordering, then agreement

Consensus is two problems, not one. First, ordering: in what sequence did transactions arrive? Second, agreement: which sequence does every honest node adopt? Proof-of-work answers both with a lottery — the winner of the hash lottery proposes the next block, and the chain with the most accumulated work wins. Proof-of-stake replaces the lottery with stake-weighted leadership. BFT protocols (Tendermint, Snow, SCP) answer with voting rounds among known validator sets. Federated agreement (Stellar) lets each node pick whom it trusts and derives network-wide agreement from overlapping trust. None is "best"; each buys its properties — permissionlessness, finality speed, energy use, liveness under attack — at a price.

Money at the protocol layer

A subtle distinction runs through this track: some systems put money in the protocol (Bitcoin's UTXO set, XRPL's XRP balances and issued currencies, Stellar's native assets), while others put money beside the protocol — ISO 20022, for example, is not a ledger at all but a messaging standard that describes payment instructions moving between banks. Understanding which layer a technology lives on is the single fastest way to cut through crypto marketing: a "compliant coin" claim usually confuses the message format a bank speaks with the ledger that settles value.

The curriculum

Fourteen modules, from the genesis block to the custody vault.

Module 1

Bitcoin — the UTXO protocol

Bitcoin's whitepaper opens with a single sentence that states the whole program: "A purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash would allow online payments to be sent directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution." The engineering underneath is the UTXO model: there are no accounts at the protocol layer, only unspent transaction outputs — discrete coins, each locked by a script puzzle. A transaction consumes old outputs as inputs (referenced by txid + output index, the outpoint) and creates new outputs; the fee is simply the implicit difference between inputs and outputs. Double-spending is prevented because the network's shared rulebook — enforced by every full node independently — rejects any transaction that spends an already-spent outpoint, and proof-of-work decides which history of spends wins. Mining is a hash lottery: miners grind a block header's nonce until its hash falls below a target, and the difficulty retargets every 2016 blocks to hold the average near ten minutes regardless of hashrate. Transactions carry a stack-based Script (no loops): the locking script on an output and the unlocking script on the spending input execute together, which is what "ownership" reduces to. SegWit moved signature (witness) data out of the legacy size accounting, fixing transaction malleability and enabling new address types; Taproot (Schnorr signatures, key-path and script-path spending) made complex spends look like ordinary single-key payments. Keys live on the secp256k1 curve, wallets derive trees of keys from one seed (BIP32/BIP39/BIP44), and addresses are just encodings of public-key hashes with checksums. Above the chain, the Lightning Network moves payments off-chain: two parties lock funds in a 2-of-2 multisig channel, exchange signed commitment transactions that update balances without touching the chain, and route multi-hop payments with hash time-locked contracts (HTLCs) — the preimage of a hash proving payment across strangers. For the deep version of this material, see the companion study guide.

Lab: run Bitcoin Core on regtest, mine blocks to an address you control, and trace one coin from coinbase output through three spends with decoderawtransaction; then build a P2SH 2-of-3 multisig by hand and spend it, and open a two-node regtest Lightning channel and route an off-chain payment. Self-check: given a 0.5 BTC input and outputs of 0.3 + 0.15 BTC, where did the rest go? Why can a consensus-valid transaction still be rejected by a node's mempool policy? What stops an old revoked Lightning commitment from being broadcast profitably? Resources: the Bitcoin whitepaper (bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf), the Bitcoin developer documentation (developer.bitcoin.org), learnmeabitcoin.com — all free. Companion: Bitcoin Protocol Engineering study sequence.

Module 2

Ethereum — accounts and the EVM

Ethereum keeps accounts, not UTXOs: every address is either an externally owned account (EOA, controlled by a private key) or a contract account (controlled by code), each with a balance, a nonce, and — for contracts — code and storage. State transitions happen when transactions are applied to this global state, and the machine that applies them is the Ethereum Virtual Machine: a stack-based, deterministic, gas-metered computer replicated on every node. Gas is the metering that makes an open world computer survivable — every opcode costs gas, the transaction sets a gas limit and a fee (max fee per gas plus priority fee), and execution that runs out of gas reverts. Storage is the expensive resource: 32-byte slots, tightly packed, with mappings and dynamic arrays hashed into pseudo-random slot addresses; memory and calldata are cheap, volatile, and per-call. Contract calls are just transactions whose data field begins with a 4-byte function selector (the first bytes of the keccak256 hash of the function signature) followed by ABI-encoded arguments. Nonces order each account's transactions and prevent replays. The deep engineering skill here is reading state: knowing exactly which storage slot a variable lives in, what a raw transaction's input data decodes to, and where the gas went in a trace.

Lab: on a local EVM (e.g. Anvil), hand-encode a function selector and arguments, send a raw transaction, and decode a mainnet transaction's input data against its contract ABI; deploy a contract with packed storage variables and read the raw slots to verify the packing; replay an out-of-gas transaction and account for every unit of gas in the trace. Self-check: why does a contract account's nonce stay 1 while an EOA's increments per transaction? Compute the maximum fee of a transaction from maxFeePerGas and gas limit. Why are storage writes the dominant cost in any contract? Resources: ethereum.org developer documentation, the Solidity documentation's EVM sections — all free.

Module 3

Solidity and smart-contract security

Solidity compiles to EVM bytecode, and its semantics are the attack surface. The vulnerability classes repeat across every major exploit: reentrancy, where an external call hands control to an attacker contract that re-enters before state is updated — the checks-effects-interactions ordering and pull-payment patterns exist to kill it; access-control failures, from missing onlyOwner-style modifiers to tx.origin authentication that breaks the moment a user acts through any intermediary contract; delegatecall hazards, where callee code executes in the caller's storage context so a malicious implementation can overwrite the owner's slot; oracle manipulation, where a lending protocol priced off a single DEX spot quote is drained in one block by flash capital — defended with TWAPs, multi-source aggregation, staleness checks, and circuit breakers; front-running and MEV, where the public mempool lets attackers sandwich your trade; and signature replay, where an omitted chain ID or nonce lets a signed authorization be reused on another chain. Arithmetic overflow is largely historical — Solidity 0.8 made checked math the default — but precision loss and rounding direction still move real money. The professional workflow is layered: unit and fork tests, static analyzers triaged into true and false positives, stateful fuzzing with invariants (e.g. total assets must equal the sum of user shares), then a scoped audit — threat model first, findings written with severity, proof of concept, impact, and remediation — and finally deployment hygiene: testnet staging, exact source verification on the explorer, hardware-wallet admin keys, multisig thresholds, timelocks, pausable guards, and an incident-response plan. OpenZeppelin's audited contracts (ERC-20, ERC-721, AccessControl, upgradeable proxies) exist so you never hand-roll token logic.

Lab: write a vulnerable withdraw function, exploit it with an attacker contract on a local chain, then fix it with checks-effects-interactions and a reentrancy guard; build a spot-price lending contract, manipulate it in one transaction on a forked mainnet, then add a TWAP feed and watch the attack fail; run a static analyzer over your contracts and classify each finding. Self-check: trace the exact call sequence of a reentrancy exploit against a withdraw-before-update function. Why is a single-block spot price unsafe, and what does a TWAP change about the attacker's cost? Name the five required sections of a complete audit finding. Resources: docs.soliditylang.org, docs.openzeppelin.com, ethereum.org developer security sections — all free. Companion: Solidity and Smart Contract Security study sequence.

Module 4

The XRP Ledger — consensus, payments, issued currencies

The XRP Ledger (XRPL) is a payments-first ledger that closes a new ledger roughly every three to five seconds. Its consensus protocol does not mine: a set of validators each maintain a Unique Node List (UNL) of validators they trust, propose transaction sets, and iterate toward agreement until a supermajority validates the same ledger — no proof-of-work, no staking, finality in seconds. Accounts are key pairs (secp256k1 or ed25519); the AccountRoot object holds the XRP balance, a strictly incrementing sequence number (gaps and replays are rejected), and a count of owned ledger objects. Every account must hold a base reserve of XRP plus an owner reserve per ledger object it owns — an economic spam defense that makes creating trust lines and offers cost real money. Signing happens client-side over a canonical binary serialization; transactions declare a fee (quoted in drops, millionths of an XRP) and a LastLedgerSequence that bounds their retry window, and results are classified as applied (tesSUCCESS), claimed-cost failures (tec), malformed (tem), or retryable (ter). The ledger's killer features are native to the protocol: a decentralized exchange where offers are first-class ledger objects matched at quality, with autobridging through XRP; pathfinding that routes a payment across trust lines and order books in one atomic transaction; and issued currencies (IOUs) that exist only where a trust line authorizes a holder to carry an issuer's currency up to a limit — the mechanism gateways use to represent dollars, euros, or any asset on-ledger, with freeze, clawback, and require-auth controls for regulated issuance. XLS-20 added native NFTs (taxon, transfer fees, brokered offers) and XLS-30 added on-ledger automated market makers with LP tokens and a fee-auction slot. Client development runs through the official xrpl.js and xrpl-py libraries over WebSocket, with Testnet and Devnet faucets for safe practice.

Lab: generate wallets offline, fund them from the Testnet faucet, and run a full issue-trust-pay-settle cycle: open a trust line, issue an IOU, post DEX offers, and execute a cross-currency path payment with SendMax while checking delivered_amount; then mint an XLS-20 NFT and create an XLS-30 AMM pool. Self-check: an account owns 3 trust lines and 2 open offers — write the reserve formula. Why can an account never spend its base reserve, and what attack does that prevent? What is the difference between a tec-class and a ter-class result for fee accounting? Resources: xrpl.org documentation (free and comprehensive), the xrpl.js and xrpl-py repositories. Companion: XRP Ledger Engineering study sequence.

Module 5

ISO 20022 — banking's messaging standard

ISO 20022 is not a blockchain, a coin, or a settlement network. It is the global standard for financial messaging: a methodology and a message catalogue that defines how banks describe payment instructions, in structured XML (the "MX" format), so that money can move between institutions with straight-through processing instead of humans reading free-text fields. Its predecessor, SWIFT's MT ("Message Type") format, carried numbered fields with largely free-text content — an MT103 for a customer credit transfer, an MT202 for a bank-to-bank transfer, an MT940 for a statement. The MX equivalents are richer and machine-readable: pain.001 (payment initiation, customer to bank), pacs.008 (FI-to-FI customer credit transfer), pacs.009 (bank-to-bank transfer), camt.053 (account statement), camt.054 (debit/credit notification), pacs.002/pain.002 (status reports), pacs.003 (direct debit). The industry has spent the 2020s migrating from MT to MX — SWIFT's CBPR+ program moved cross-border payment instructions onto MX, and the data richness of pacs.008 (structured remittance, purpose codes, party identification) is what enables modern sanctions screening and reconciliation. Where does crypto intersect it? At the messaging layer, not the settlement layer: a bank whose rails speak ISO 20022 can instruct payments that eventually settle on any ledger, and payment products that bridge crypto to banking must parse and emit these messages. This is the precise point where marketing routinely overreaches — community claims that specific cryptocurrencies are "ISO 20022 compliant" are unverified unless confirmed by the standards body itself, because ISO 20022 standardizes messages between institutions, not coins. The engineering skill is reading the boundary: build a pain.001-to-pacs.008 field map and you understand what banks actually standardized; believe a coin is "compliant" and you have confused the envelope for the ledger.

Lab: hand-write a minimal pain.001 credit-transfer-initiation XML for a fictional payment and map each of its debtor/creditor/amount fields to the corresponding pacs.008 elements a bank would emit downstream; then write the MT103-to-pacs.008 field mapping table and note which MX data has no MT analogue (structured remittance, purpose codes). Self-check: name the MX equivalents of MT103, MT202, and MT940. What does ISO 20022 standardize, and what does it explicitly not standardize? Why is "coin X is ISO 20022 compliant" a category error? Resources: iso20022.org (the standards body), SWIFT's ISO 20022 migration documentation — free.

Module 6

Monero — privacy by protocol

Where Bitcoin is pseudonymous — every amount, sender, and receiver visible on a public graph — Monero (XMR) makes privacy mandatory and protocol-level, so that no user has to opt in and no transaction stands out for being private. Four mechanisms compose the shield. Stealth addresses hide the recipient: for every payment, the sender derives a one-time address from the recipient's public keys, so the chain never shows that a particular person's address received funds — only that some one-time address did, which the recipient recognizes with their private view key. Ring signatures hide the sender: the real spend is mixed with decoy outputs drawn from the chain's history, and an observer can verify that one of the ring's members signed without learning which — the current default ring size is 16, one real input among fifteen decoys. RingCT (ring confidential transactions) hides the amount: Pedersen commitments let nodes verify that inputs equal outputs without seeing the numbers, while Bulletproofs+ range proofs guarantee each hidden amount is non-negative so no money is created from nothing. Dandelion++ hardens the network layer: a new transaction first travels a random "stem" of single-node relays before probabilistically entering the normal broadcast ("fluff"), making it harder to tie a transaction to the IP address that originated it. Consensus is proof-of-work with RandomX, deliberately CPU-oriented to keep mining on commodity hardware, plus a tail emission of 0.6 XMR per block so miners are paid forever rather than relying on fees alone. The trade-offs are real and worth stating plainly: transactions are much heavier than Bitcoin's, the chain is harder to audit or reconcile with compliance tooling, and network-layer privacy still needs Tor for full protection — Dandelion++ obscures the origin from casual observers, not from a determined network adversary. Monero originated in 2014 from the CryptoNote codebase, not from Bitcoin's, and remains the reference design for mandatory on-chain privacy.

Lab: run a Monero node on stagenet, create a stagenet wallet, receive test XMR from a faucet, and inspect the resulting transaction in a stagenet explorer — write down exactly what is visible (fees, ring members, one-time addresses) and what is hidden (real sender, real recipient, amounts); then compare that explorer view, field by field, with a Bitcoin testnet transaction. Self-check: explain how a ring signature obscures the true signer without any trusted setup. What does a stealth address protect, and what does it not protect? Why does RingCT need range proofs in addition to commitments? Resources: getmonero.org documentation and Moneropedia, the Monero technical specifications in the monero-docs repository — all free and primary.

Module 7

Stellar — federated agreement and anchors

Stellar (XLM) is a payments-first ledger that reaches consensus without mining or staking, through the Stellar Consensus Protocol (SCP) — an implementation of federated Byzantine agreement. Each validator independently chooses a quorum slice: the set of other validators it trusts to convince it. A node accepts a statement when one of its slices unanimously agrees; because slices overlap across the network, local agreements cascade into network-wide agreement. SCP runs in two phases per ledger slot: nomination, where nodes propose and federated-vote candidate transaction sets into a composite value, and the ballot protocol, a prepare/confirm/externalize commit sequence with monotonically increasing ballot numbers that drives the network to a single decided value. The design's honesty is its documented trade-off: safety and liveness hinge on slice choice — slices with poor overlap can fork, while oversized slices stall when members go down, so the Stellar Development Foundation publishes guidance on choosing slices with real-world-rooted trust. On top of this consensus, Stellar is built as money infrastructure: anyone can issue assets with trustlines (a holder opts into carrying an issuer's asset, mirroring the XRPL design), a native on-ledger DEX matches orders, and path payments atomically convert between assets in a single transaction so a sender can pay in dollars and the recipient receive euros. Anchors are the bridge to the real world: regulated institutions that take deposits off-chain and issue corresponding tokens on-chain (and redeem them back), following the SEP (Stellar Ecosystem Proposal) standards — the mechanism by which fiat like USDC exists natively on the network. Soroban is Stellar's smart-contract platform: contracts written in Rust, compiled to WASM, deployed on-chain with a fee and resource model designed for the ledger's payments workload rather than a general world computer. Ledgers close in seconds, fees are a fraction of a cent, and the whole architecture optimizes for one job: moving value across borders and currencies cheaply.

Lab: on Stellar testnet, create and fund two accounts, issue a custom asset from one, establish a trustline from the other, and execute a path payment between two different assets while tracing the route; then write, deploy, and invoke a minimal Soroban contract in Rust on testnet. Self-check: what is a quorum slice, and why does network-wide agreement depend on slices overlapping? Contrast federated voting with classic BFT quorum voting. What does an anchor do that a DEX cannot? Resources: developers.stellar.org (free, primary), the SCP whitepaper by David Mazières, the stellar-core repository.

Module 8

Protocol families I — proof-of-work and its performance descendants

Proof-of-work did not end with Bitcoin; it diversified. Litecoin (LTC) is the closest descendant: the same UTXO architecture and halving-style issuance (capped at 84 million, four times Bitcoin's), but with the Scrypt hash function — chosen as memory-hard so that mining favored memory bandwidth over raw hashing speed — and a 2.5-minute block cadence for faster first confirmations. Litecoin also served as Bitcoin's live testbed for SegWit, and its MWEB (Mimblewimble Extension Blocks) upgrade added optional confidential transactions via extension blocks, with peg-in/peg-out mechanics between the transparent chain and the confidential extension. Its trade-offs are Bitcoin's trade-offs at smaller scale: a smaller hashrate means a cheaper majority attack, and merged-mining dynamics tie its security partly to miners who primarily mine elsewhere. Solana (SOL) takes the opposite branch — keeping high throughput rather than Bitcoin's conservatism. Its headline innovation is Proof of History: a verifiable delay function producing a cryptographic clock, a sequential hash chain that timestamps events so validators can agree on order before they agree on state, which lets the network pipeline transaction processing. Finality comes from Tower BFT, a PBFT-like vote-based protocol layered over the PoH clock, and execution runs on Sealevel, a parallel runtime that processes non-overlapping transactions concurrently — the reason Solana transactions must declare every account they will touch upfront. Accounts are state containers rented with lamports (rent), and programs (Solana's name for contracts) are stateless, typically written with the Anchor framework. The family lesson: PoW buys permissionless leader election at the cost of energy and slow finality; Solana's hybrid buys throughput at the cost of demanding validator hardware and a more complex failure surface, including the account-validation bugs that are its signature security consideration.

Lab: run solana-test-validator locally, airdrop test SOL, and trace a devnet transaction's account inputs and program invocations in the Solana explorer; then scaffold an Anchor counter program, deploy it to devnet, and call it from a small client — and deliberately omit a required account to read the validation error. Self-check: what problem does Proof of History solve that plain proof-of-stake does not? Why must a Solana transaction declare all accounts it touches? Contrast Litecoin's confirmation profile with Bitcoin's. Resources: solana.com/developers, litecoin.com — free.

Module 9

Protocol families II — proof-of-stake variants

Proof-of-stake replaces the mining lottery with leadership weighted by locked capital, but the implementations diverge sharply. Cardano (ADA) runs Ouroboros, a provably secure PoS protocol that divides time into slots and epochs, elects slot leaders proportionally to stake, and lets holders delegate to stake pools without surrendering custody. Its ledger model is the extended UTXO (eUTxO): like Bitcoin's outputs, but each output can carry a datum (contract state) and transactions carry redeemers (the data a validator script checks), so contracts execute with Bitcoin-like deterministic validation — a script sees only the transaction it validates, which eliminates whole classes of reentrancy-style bugs at the cost of a harder programming model. Scripts are written in Plutus, and assets are native: tokens live on the ledger itself, identified by policy ID, rather than inside contract code. Polygon's PoS chain (POL) takes the pragmatic route: a two-layer design where the Bor layer produces blocks rapidly and the Heimdall validator layer checkpoints state back to Ethereum, borrowing Ethereum's security for finality while running its own validator set for speed — plus the zkEVM concept, a zero-knowledge rollup approach that proves EVM execution with validity proofs rather than trusting validators. The family trade-offs: Cardano buys formal verification and deterministic execution at the cost of ecosystem velocity; checkpointed sidechains buy EVM compatibility and speed at the cost of a validator set and bridge trust assumptions that a rollup's mathematics would not need.

Lab: on a Cardano testnet, build and submit a plain ADA transfer with cardano-cli, observing inputs, outputs, and fees; deploy an always-succeeds Plutus validator and redeem it, then contrast the transaction's structure with an account-model transfer. On Polygon's Amoy testnet, deploy a Solidity contract, verify it on the explorer, and trace a checkpoint transaction back to Ethereum. Self-check: what does eUTxO add on top of classic UTXO for smart contracts? Explain the roles of Heimdall and Bor. Contrast a checkpointed PoS chain's security model with a zero-knowledge rollup's. Resources: developers.cardano.org, docs.polygon.technology — free.

Module 10

Protocol families III — BFT interoperability: Cosmos, Polkadot, Avalanche

Three ecosystems bet that the future is many chains, not one — and each invented its own way for chains to talk. Cosmos (ATOM) is built on Tendermint BFT: a known validator set proposes and votes blocks in rounds, with instant finality once two-thirds precommit — no forks under normal operation, at the cost of requiring that known, staked set. The Cosmos SDK composes application logic from modules (bank, staking, governance), and IBC (Inter-Blockchain Communication) moves tokens and data between chains over light-client-verified channels — the channel handshake, not blind message passing, is what makes the transfer trust-minimized, with relayers carrying packets but unable to forge them. Polkadot (DOT) centralizes security instead of sovereignty: a relay chain provides shared validation while parachains plug in to borrow it, selected through slot auctions; nominated proof-of-stake lets token holders back validators; and XCM is a message format for cross-consensus communication — chains telling each other what to do, not just bridging tokens. Avalanche (AVAX) replaces quorum voting with the Snow family's metastable consensus: validators repeatedly sample small random subsets of peers and converge on the majority opinion, which is fast and scales to large validator sets. Its primary network splits duties across three chains — X for assets, P for platform/staking, C for EVM contracts — and subnets let anyone spin up an application-specific validator set with its own virtual machine. The family trade-offs: Tendermint buys instant finality and sovereignty at the cost of validator-set management and slashing risk; Polkadot buys shared security at the cost of slot scarcity and relay-chain governance capture; Avalanche buys scalability and customization at the cost of subnet incentive design and cross-chain atomicity questions.

Lab: scaffold a single-node Cosmos chain with the Ignite CLI and submit a transaction to your own module; on a public testnet, inspect IBC channel state with an explorer or CLI query and diagram a packet's lifecycle. Run a local Substrate node template and inspect its runtime pallets via Polkadot.js Apps. On Avalanche's Fuji testnet, move test AVAX between the X, P, and C chains and observe the export/import flow. Self-check: why do IBC channels, rather than direct message passing, connect two Cosmos chains? How does shared security differ from each parachain securing itself? Contrast Snow-family subsampled voting with classic BFT quorum voting. Resources: docs.cosmos.network, docs.polkadot.com, docs.avax.network — free.

Module 11

Protocol families IV — beyond the chain: hashgraph and oracles

Not every distributed ledger is a blockchain. Hedera (HBAR) runs on hashgraph: instead of blocks, nodes gossip events about events — "gossip about gossip" — so every node independently reconstructs the full history of who told whom what, when. From that shared causal graph, nodes compute consensus virtually: because everyone holds the same graph, each node can simulate how every other node would vote, so no votes are actually exchanged — a design with asynchronous Byzantine fault tolerance and fast finality. Hedera exposes its ledger as services: the Consensus Service (ordered, timestamped messages), the Token Service (native tokenization), and an EVM-equivalent Smart Contract Service; consensus nodes order transactions while mirror nodes serve history. Its distinctive trade-off is governance: the validator set is council-governed, which buys predictable performance and enterprise comfort at the cost of the permissionlessness that PoW and open PoS offer. Chainlink (LINK) solves a different problem — blockchains cannot see the outside world, so decentralized oracle networks aggregate off-chain data (prices, randomness via VRF, automation triggers) through off-chain reporting with on-chain verification: no single node is trusted, the quorum's signed report is. CCIP extends the same trust model to cross-chain messaging. The family lesson is architectural: hashgraph shows consensus need not be block-shaped, and oracles show that a ledger's security perimeter ends at its data inputs — the oracle-manipulation exploits of Module 3 are this family's dark side, and stale feeds, source manipulation, and cross-chain finality are its standing risks.

Lab: on Hedera testnet, create a fungible token with the Token Service, transfer it between accounts, then submit a message to the Consensus Service and retrieve it via a mirror node query. Deploy a consumer contract on a testnet that reads a Chainlink price feed, then add a staleness check and explain what breaks without it. Self-check: explain gossip-about-gossip and why nodes never need to exchange votes. What makes an oracle report trustworthy when no single node is trusted? Contrast consensus nodes with mirror nodes. Resources: docs.hedera.com, docs.chain.link — free.

Module 12

Protocol families V — the payments ledgers in context

Step back and the payments corner of the top coins resolves into three deliberate philosophies, each a full answer to "how should value move?" The XRP Ledger (Module 4) bets on institutional payment flows: federated consensus for three-second settlement, issued currencies with issuer controls (freeze, clawback, require-auth) that let regulated gateways represent fiat on-ledger, and a native DEX with pathfinding so conversion is atomic. Stellar (Module 7) bets on open financial inclusion: the same federated-agreement mathematics, but with trustlines anyone can open, anchors bridging fiat in and out, and Soroban contracts for programmable money — optimized for corridors and currencies rather than counterparties. Monero (Module 6) bets on fungibility as a prerequisite for money: if every coin's history is visible, coins become tainted and unequal, so the protocol hides sender, recipient, and amount for everyone, always — at the cost of heavy transactions and deliberate distance from the compliance tooling the other two court. Around all three sits ISO 20022 (Module 5): not a competitor but the messaging layer of the banking system they all must eventually speak to, where pacs.008 and camt.053 describe the same economic events these ledgers settle. The family trade-off table is therefore a product question, not a technical one: regulated corridors want issuer controls and messaging compatibility; open networks want permissionless issuance and tiny fees; privacy-first users want mandatory confidentiality. A settlement product that cannot say which of these it is has not chosen a protocol — it has chosen a press release.

Lab: build a comparison matrix of the XRP Ledger, Stellar, and Monero across six axes — consensus mechanism, ledger model, settlement time, fee model, privacy guarantees, and issuer/regulatory controls — citing one primary documentation page per cell; then write a one-page memo recommending one of the three for a remittance corridor and one for a regulated stablecoin, with the trade-off you are accepting in each case. Self-check: which of the three ledgers gives an issuer the ability to freeze balances, and why would the other two consider that a bug? Where does ISO 20022 sit relative to all three — above, below, or beside them? Resources: xrpl.org, developers.stellar.org, getmonero.org, iso20022.org — all free. Companion: Ledger Protocol Engineering study sequence.

Module 13

Wallets, keys, and custody engineering

Every ledger in this track reduces, at the edge, to one brutal fact: whoever holds the private key controls the funds, and there is no password reset. Custody engineering is the discipline of managing that fact at scale. It starts with hierarchical deterministic wallets: one seed (usually a BIP39 mnemonic) derives a tree of keys via BIP32, with hardened derivation protecting parent keys from child-key leaks, and account paths (BIP44/48/84) organizing keys by coin and purpose — one backup secures everything, which is why the seed's physical security is the whole game. Multisig distributes control: an m-of-n script (or a smart-contract wallet, or a threshold signature) means no single key can move funds, so theft or loss of one key is survivable. MPC (multi-party computation) and HSMs move this into institutions: key shares that never reconstruct in one place, signing inside tamper-resistant hardware, with quorum policies enforced by software rather than trust. Operationally, the layers are: hot wallets (online, small balances, automated), warm wallets (online but quorum-gated), and cold storage (offline, air-gapped signing, geographically distributed backups); the catastrophic failures in this industry's history are almost never cryptography — they are a single hot key with no quorum, a seed photographed into a cloud backup, a deployer key and an admin key being the same key. Signing hygiene completes the picture: verify what you sign on a trusted display, never paste seeds into networked machines, separate deployer from admin from treasury roles, and rehearse recovery — a backup you have never restored is a rumor. Custody is where protocol theory meets the oldest security truth: the system is only as strong as the humans holding the keys.

Lab: in an offline environment, generate a BIP39 seed, derive three receiving addresses at different paths, and document the backup-and-restore procedure you would hand to a non-technical inheritor; then set up a 2-of-3 multisig on a testnet, lose one key on purpose, and recover spending ability with the remaining two — writing the incident report as if real funds were involved. Self-check: why is sharing an xpub with a third party a privacy leak? What breaks if hardened derivation is not used between a parent and child key? Name the three roles that should never share one private key in a production deployment. Resources: the BIP32/BIP39/BIP44 specification texts, developer.bitcoin.org wallet documentation — free.

Module 14

Capstone — the protocol-selection brief

The track converges on one deliverable, the kind a blockchain engineer actually gets asked to produce: a protocol-selection brief for a hypothetical cross-border settlement product. Pick three protocol families from this track — for example, the XRP Ledger's federated consensus, Stellar's anchor model, and a BFT-interoperability design from Module 10 — and justify your choice in writing, with diagrams, across the axes that matter: ledger model and what it implies for your product's accounting, consensus mechanism and its finality/liveness/permissionlessness trade-offs, fee economics at your expected volume, privacy and regulatory posture (issued-currency controls, auditability, messaging compatibility with ISO 20022), smart-contract needs, and custody and key-management fit. Your brief must name the trade-offs you are accepting, not just the features you are buying; it must include a failure-mode analysis (what happens when validators stall, when an oracle feed goes stale, when a key is lost); and it must survive a red-team review where a peer argues for the protocol you rejected. Alongside the brief, the track assessment: a written examination covering the full arc — decode a raw Bitcoin transaction's structure, trace a reentrancy exploit's call sequence, explain quorum slices to a skeptic, map an MT103 to its MX equivalent, and argue, from first principles, why your chosen settlement ledger beats the alternatives. This is the point of the whole track: not collecting coin names, but reasoning about ledgers like an engineer — state, transitions, ordering, agreement, and the price of every property.

Lab (capstone): write the protocol-selection brief (2,000–3,000 words plus architecture diagrams), present it as a 20-minute briefing, and defend it against a red-team reviewer assigned the protocol you rejected; then sit the track assessment under timed conditions. Self-check: can you state, in one paragraph each, the consensus mechanism, ledger model, and central trade-off of all ten protocol families? If not, loop back before the assessment. Corrections

The legends, graded.

"This coin is ISO 20022 compliant." Category error. ISO 20022 standardizes messages between financial institutions — the XML envelopes banks use to instruct payments — not coins, ledgers, or tokens. The standards body does not certify cryptocurrencies. A payment company can build ISO 20022 messaging around any ledger; no ledger is compliant or non-compliant in itself. Treat the claim as marketing unless it describes a specific messaging integration.

"Blockchain transactions are anonymous." Nuance, and the nuance matters. Bitcoin and Ethereum are pseudonymous: addresses are not names, but every amount and every hop is public forever, and clustering analysis routinely de-anonymizes flows. Monero is private by protocol — sender, recipient, and amount hidden for every transaction — but even there, operational mistakes (a KYC exchange, a leaked IP) can link a user to their funds. "Anonymous" is a claim about your whole operational posture, not a property a ledger grants you.

"Smart contracts are contracts." Misleading. A smart contract is code that executes deterministically; whether it constitutes a legally enforceable agreement depends on jurisdiction, the parties' intent, and often a separate legal wrapper. The DAO-era lesson stands: "code is law" is a design philosophy, not a legal doctrine, and the exploits in Module 3 are what happens when the distinction is ignored.

"Proof-of-work is unhackable." Loose. Majority-hashrate attacks are not theoretical — smaller proof-of-work chains have suffered them, with attackers reorganizing history and double-spending. Security in PoW is economic, not absolute: it holds while attacking costs more than it pays. Bitcoin's hashrate makes that math punishing; a small chain's does not.

"Lose your keys and support can recover them." False for self-custody, and the most expensive misunderstanding in the field. On every ledger in this track, the key is the account. No help desk, no validator, no foundation can move funds without it. Custodial services can recover access because they hold the keys for you — which is exactly the trade-off Module 13 teaches you to price.

"Newer consensus is strictly better than proof-of-work." False framing. Every mechanism in Modules 8–11 buys its properties at a price: BFT buys finality with validator-set trust, federated agreement buys speed with slice-configuration risk, hashgraph buys performance with council governance. "Better" is meaningless without the workload: settlement finality, permissionless entry, energy budget, and regulatory posture point at different winners.
