SCIENTIA OMNIA VINCIT

The track

A computer that plays by different rules.

A classical bit is a switch: 0 or 1. A qubit is a vector — a combination of 0 and 1 with complex-valued weights — and a register of n qubits lives in a space of 2n dimensions. Operations on qubits are rotations of those vectors, measurement converts amplitudes into probabilities, and two qubits can be entangled so that neither has a state of its own. That is the entire mathematical game, and it is enough to make certain problems — factoring large integers, searching an unsorted list — asymptotically cheaper than any known classical method.

Those two sentences are where honesty must begin. The mathematics of quantum computing is settled; its engineering is not. Nobody owns a quantum computer that factors the RSA keys protecting your bank login, and the machines that do exist are noisy, short-lived in their quantum states, and available mostly through cloud queues. This track teaches the settled part with real mathematics and real runnable code, and the unsettled part with vendor claims explicitly labeled as vendor claims.

You will compute with state vectors on paper, write your first quantum circuit in Qiskit and run it on a simulator, understand why Shor's algorithm breaks RSA in principle, know which NIST standards replace the vulnerable cryptography, and finish by doing the most useful skill in this field: reading a "quantum advantage" headline and deciding whether it means anything.

Chronology

Forty-six years from suggestion to standards.

  1. 1980

    Physicist Paul Benioff describes a quantum Turing machine; Richard Feynman proposes simulating quantum systems with quantum computers.

  2. 1985

    David Deutsch publishes the quantum Turing machine and the first quantum algorithm — the ancestor of Deutsch-Jozsa.

  3. 1992

    The Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm demonstrates a provable exponential gap between quantum and classical queries for a specific problem.

  4. 1994

    Peter Shor publishes a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for factoring and discrete logarithms — the result that would eventually force the world's cryptography to migrate.

  5. 1996

    Lov Grover publishes his quantum search algorithm: quadratic speedup for unstructured search.

  6. 2016

    IBM puts a 5-qubit quantum processor on the public cloud — the start of cloud quantum access.

  7. 2019

    Google claims "quantum supremacy" with its 53-qubit Sycamore processor on random-circuit sampling; the claim is disputed and partially closed by improved classical simulations in subsequent years.

  8. 2022

    The Nobel Prize in Physics goes to Alain Aspect, John Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger for experiments with entangled photons — testing Bell inequalities and establishing the non-classicality of entanglement.

  9. 2024

    August 13 — NIST finalizes the first post-quantum cryptography standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA). The migration of the world's public-key infrastructure begins.

  10. 2026

    State of the field: no fault-tolerant quantum computer exists; the largest processors (e.g., IBM's 156-qubit Heron line) are noisy machines used mostly for research; error-correction demonstrations are scaling but remain many orders of magnitude from cryptographically relevant sizes.

The machinery

The ideas, stated plainly.

The qubit — a vector, not a switch

A qubit's state is written |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, where α and β are complex numbers called amplitudes, constrained by |α|² + |β|² = 1. This is not "being 0 and 1 at the same time" in any useful sense — it is a precise geometric statement: the qubit points somewhere on the Bloch sphere, a unit sphere whose north pole is |0⟩ and south pole is |1⟩. Every single-qubit operation is a rotation of that arrow. You can read more: the IBM Quantum Learning site (learn.qiskit.org) covers this free and open.

The Born rule — how measurement pays out

Measurement is the one place where quantum computing stops being reversible geometry and becomes probability. Measuring |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ returns 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|², and the state collapses to the outcome observed. Nothing about the state can be read directly — a million measurements of identically prepared qubits reveal the probabilities, never the amplitudes themselves. This single fact explains why quantum computers cannot simply "try all answers at once": you can compute on all possibilities in superposition, but reading the answer out only reveals one sample drawn from a probability distribution.

Multi-qubit states — where the power hides

Two qubits do not hold "two superpositions" — they hold one vector in a four-dimensional space: α₀₀|00⟩ + α₀₁|01⟩ + α₁₀|10⟩ + α₁₁|11⟩, with the squares of the amplitudes summing to 1. For n qubits, the state vector has 2n components. This exponential dimension is the entire reason quantum computing is interesting and also the entire reason it is hard to simulate classically. Entanglement (Module 2) is the phenomenon that some of these joint states cannot be split into independent single-qubit descriptions at all.

Why interference is the real trick

Quantum algorithms work by arranging interference: amplitudes corresponding to wrong answers cancel each other out (destructive interference) while amplitudes for the right answer reinforce (constructive interference), so the final measurement samples the desired answer with high probability. Every algorithm in this track — Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover, Shor — is an interference pattern designed by hand. The qubit itself is trivial; the interference choreography is the art.

No-cloning — you cannot copy a qubit

There is no operation that copies an unknown quantum state |ψ⟩ into |ψ⟩|ψ⟩. This is a theorem (proved 1982), not a hardware limitation, and it is why quantum computing feels so alien: no variables can be copied, no state can be inspected mid-computation, and error correction must protect quantum information without ever reading it. It is also the bedrock of quantum cryptography.

Decoherence — why the machines are hard

A qubit only stays quantum while it is isolated. Interaction with its environment — heat, stray electromagnetic fields, even the control wiring itself — collapses superpositions toward classical noise. This is decoherence. The headline hardware metrics all describe the fight against it: T1 (how long a qubit holds its energy state), T2 (how long it holds phase coherence), and gate error rates (how much noise each operation adds). Every platform's engineering is a different answer to the same question: how do you keep a fragile quantum state alive long enough to finish the computation?

The curriculum

Ten modules, from the qubit to the verdict.

Module 1

Qubits, superposition, and measurement

The state vector |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ and the normalization constraint |α|² + |β|² = 1; the Born rule (probabilities are squared amplitudes); collapse on measurement; the Bloch sphere as a picture of every single-qubit state; multi-qubit state spaces and why they grow as 2n; interference as the core algorithmic resource. Worked by hand: computing measurement probabilities for |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩.

Hands-on: pick five random points on the Bloch sphere and compute their |0⟩-measurement probabilities by hand. Self-check: why can't you determine α and β from a single measurement? What does normalization physically guarantee? Resources: IBM Quantum Learning (learn.qiskit.org) single-qubit course; Nielsen & Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information, ch. 1–2 (standard university text).

Module 2

Entanglement and Bell-type correlations

The four Bell states, starting with (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 — maximally entangled pairs whose measurement outcomes are perfectly correlated yet individually random; why entangled states cannot be factored into independent single-qubit states; Bell's 1964 theorem at the conceptual level (no local hidden-variable theory reproduces quantum predictions); the experimental record — Bell-inequality tests culminating in the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics to Aspect, Clauser, and Zeilinger; entanglement as a resource for teleportation and superdense coding, and why it still cannot send messages faster than light.

Hands-on: write the measurement table for the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 and show that Alice's outcome predicts Bob's but neither is predictable alone. Self-check: explain why entanglement correlations do not violate special relativity. Resources: the Qiskit textbook (learn.qiskit.org) entanglement chapter; the Nobel Prize organization's summary of the 2022 physics prize.

Module 3

Quantum gates and circuits

Gates as unitary rotations: Pauli-X (NOT), Hadamard (creates superposition), phase gates S and T, and the two-qubit CNOT (flip the target if the control is 1) — the gate that builds entanglement; the no-cloning theorem as a design constraint; universality — the standard result that the gate set {H, T, CNOT} can approximate any quantum computation, so a small gate vocabulary suffices; circuit diagrams as the assembly language of the field; depth, width, and why both are limited by decoherence on real hardware.

Hands-on: draw the circuit that builds (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 from |00⟩ (H on qubit 0, then CNOT 0→1) and trace the state vector after each gate. Self-check: why is {H, T, CNOT} universal while classical {AND, NOT} is not enough for quantum? Resources: Qiskit documentation's circuit library reference; learn.qiskit.org gates course.

Module 4

The Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm

The problem: a function on n bits is promised to be either constant (all outputs equal) or balanced (exactly half 0, half 1) — determine which. Classically, the worst case needs 2n−1 + 1 evaluations; Deutsch-Jozsa needs exactly one quantum evaluation. The walkthrough: prepare n qubits in superposition, query the function once in a way that writes its answer into the phases of the amplitudes (phase kickback), then interfere the amplitudes so constant functions collapse to |00…0⟩ and balanced ones never do. The catch, stated honestly: the problem is contrived — its value is conceptual, proving a quantum computer can beat classical computing exponentially at something, not that the something matters.

Hands-on: implement the 2-qubit Deutsch-Jozsa circuit in Qiskit's simulator for a constant and a balanced oracle. Self-check: where does the speedup come from — parallelism or interference? (Answer: interference; "trying all inputs at once" alone explains nothing.) Resources: learn.qiskit.org Deutsch-Jozsa tutorial; Nielsen & Chuang ch. 1.4.

Module 5

Grover's search — the quadratic speedup

The problem: find the one marked item among N unstructured entries. Classically: ~N/2 checks on average. Grover: ~√N iterations of a two-step dance — the oracle flips the phase of the marked item's amplitude, then the "diffusion" step reflects all amplitudes about their average, amplifying the marked one each round until measurement finds it. What it does and doesn't imply: quadratic means a 128-bit key becomes as hard as a 64-bit key — serious, but it does not break symmetric cryptography the way Shor breaks asymmetric. The standard mitigation is simply doubling key lengths (AES-128 → AES-256). Grover is optimal — no quantum algorithm searches unstructured data faster — so "quadratic" is the ceiling, not the floor.

Hands-on: code a 3-qubit Grover circuit in Qiskit for a chosen marked state and verify the marked outcome dominates the counts. Self-check: why doesn't Grover help if the data has structure you can exploit classically? Resources: learn.qiskit.org Grover tutorial; the original framing in Nielsen & Chuang ch. 6.

Module 6

Shor's algorithm — period-finding breaks RSA

The reduction, in plain steps: (1) factoring N reduces to finding the period r of the function f(x) = aˣ mod N for a random a coprime to N — a classical number-theory fact; (2) period-finding reduces to the quantum Fourier transform, which extracts periodicities from a superposition exponentially faster than classical Fourier methods; (3) from r, the factors fall out as gcd(ar/2 ± 1, N). The cryptography impact: the same period-finding breaks the discrete-logarithm problem, which underlies elliptic-curve cryptography (ECDSA, ECDH) as well as RSA and finite-field Diffie-Hellman. Two honest boundaries: Shor runs on a fault-tolerant quantum computer, and none exists — a widely cited published estimate (Gidney & Ekerå, 2019) puts breaking a 2048-bit RSA key at roughly 20 million noisy physical qubits running ~8 hours, while today's largest processors have hundreds of qubits with no error correction; and Shor does not brute-force keys, it sidesteps the hard problem entirely, so larger RSA keys are not a fix — only migration is.

Hands-on: work the classical reduction on paper: factor 15 using a=2 (period of 2ˣ mod 15 is 4, giving gcd(2²±1, 15) = 3, 5). Self-check: why does doubling RSA key size defend against Grover but not Shor? Resources: learn.qiskit.org Shor tutorial; Shor's 1994 paper via arXiv (quant-ph/9508027).

Module 7

Quantum hardware — four qubit families

Superconducting (IBM, Google, Rigetti): tiny circuits cooled near absolute zero where current loops act as qubits; IBM's Heron line is publicly described as 156-qubit processors on a heavy-hexagonal lattice, offered through cloud access — qubit counts and error rates are vendor-reported calibration snapshots that drift between systems and over time, so treat any specific figure as dated. Trapped ion (IonQ, Quantinuum): individual ions held in electromagnetic traps, manipulated with lasers — typically fewer qubits but higher gate fidelities and longer coherence, with all-to-all connectivity; vendor-claimed fidelities should be read as vendor claims. Neutral atom (Pasqal, QuEra, Atom Computing): arrays of atoms held by optical tweezers, strong in analog simulation and scaling qubit counts quickly. Photonic (PsiQuantum, Xanadu): qubits as photons, attractive for networking and room-temperature operation, but two-qubit gates are probabilistic and hard. Across all four: coherence (T1/T2 — how long quantum states survive), gate error rates (noise per operation), and the road to fault tolerance via quantum error correction — encoding one logical qubit in many physical ones (e.g., surface codes), which is where the millions-of-qubits figures for cryptography come from.

Hands-on: open IBM Quantum's public system-status page and record the qubit count, median T1/T2, and two-qubit error rate for one backend; write one paragraph on what those numbers imply for circuit depth. Self-check: why does a 1,000-qubit noisy processor not threaten RSA? Resources: IBM Quantum documentation (docs.quantum.ibm.com); vendor technology pages for IonQ, Quantinuum, Pasqal, QuEra, PsiQuantum — read as vendor material.

Module 8

Post-quantum cryptography — the migration

The threat model first: "harvest now, decrypt later" — an adversary records encrypted traffic today and decrypts it years from now once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, so data with long secrecy lifetimes (medical records, state secrets, archived intellectual property) is at risk already. The response: on August 13, 2024, NIST finalized three post-quantum standards — FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), a lattice-based key-encapsulation mechanism for key establishment (descended from CRYSTALS-Kyber); FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), a lattice-based digital signature scheme (descended from CRYSTALS-Dilithium); and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA), a hash-based signature scheme (SPHINCS+) kept as a conservative, non-lattice backup. Still in progress: FIPS 206 (FN-DSA, from FALCON) and the HQC key-encapsulation mechanism, selected for standardization in March 2025. Migration guidance (NIST SP 1800-38 and the CNSA 2.0 timeline, which targets post-quantum support in software by 2025 and exclusive use in national-security systems by 2030–2033): inventory your cryptography, prioritize by harvest-now exposure, deploy hybrid constructions (classical + post-quantum together, so a break in either leg alone doesn't compromise the session — e.g., X25519 combined with ML-KEM-768, already shipping in Chrome and Cloudflare TLS), and plan for larger keys and signatures. Grover's shadow: symmetric algorithms need no replacement, just longer keys.

Hands-on: audit one system you use — list every place public-key crypto appears (TLS, SSH keys, code signing, VPN) and rank them by data lifetime. Self-check: why is a hybrid KEM safer during transition than switching wholesale to ML-KEM? Resources: the FIPS publications at NIST CSRC (csrc.nist.gov); NIST's PQC project page; Cloudflare's blog on post-quantum TLS deployment.

Module 9

Open-source lab — Qiskit

Qiskit is IBM's open-source quantum SDK (Apache-2.0), the standard free tool for learning. Install it, build a Bell-state circuit, and run it on a local simulator — real runnable code, no quantum hardware required.

Lab 1 — install and verify. With Python 3.9+ installed: pip install qiskit qiskit-aer. (Qiskit is modular; qiskit-aer provides the high-performance simulators.) Verify: python -c "import qiskit; print(qiskit.__version__)".

Lab 2 — build and run a Bell state. Save and run the following — it constructs the entangled state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 from Module 2 and samples it 1,024 times on the Aer simulator:

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram

# H on qubit 0, then CNOT 0 -> 1: the Bell-state circuit
qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)
qc.h(0)
qc.cx(0, 1)
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])
print(qc.draw())

sim = AerSimulator()
result = sim.run(transpile(qc, sim), shots=1024).result()
counts = result.get_counts()
print(counts)          # expect ~50% '00', ~50% '11'
plot_histogram(counts) # in Jupyter: renders the distribution

Lab 3 — run on real hardware (optional). IBM's Quantum Platform offers a free access tier; after creating an account and generating an API token, the qiskit-ibm-runtime package lets you submit the same circuit to a real backend and compare the noisy counts against the simulator's ideal ones. Queues and backend availability vary; treat the free tier as a research convenience, not a guarantee.

Hands-on: modify Lab 2 to prepare (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2 instead, and predict the counts before running. Self-check: why do the simulator counts show small deviations from exactly 50/50? Resources: Qiskit documentation (docs.quantum.ibm.com); the Qiskit GitHub repository (Apache-2.0 source).

Module 10

Capstone — judge a "quantum advantage" claim

Find a recent press release or news article claiming quantum advantage, supremacy, or a breakthrough. Write a 1,000-word critical assessment using this checklist: (1) What was actually computed? — a useful problem or a benchmark designed for the hardware (e.g., random-circuit sampling)? (2) What was the classical comparison? — was the best known classical algorithm used, and did the authors give classical simulation a fair shot? Google's 2019 Sycamore claim is the textbook case study: disputed and partly closed by better classical methods afterward. (3) Error correction or error mitigation? — mitigation (cleaning up noisy results statistically) is not fault tolerance; claims that blur the two deserve skepticism. (4) Peer review vs. press release — is there a paper, or only a launch announcement? (5) Who benefits from the framing? — vendor fundraising timelines are not scientific evidence. Conclude with a verdict: verified / plausible-but-unproven / overstated, with citations.

Track assessment: (a) derive the Born-rule probabilities for a given two-qubit state vector on paper; (b) sketch the Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover, and Shor circuits from memory and state each one's speedup and its limits; (c) explain to a non-technical colleague why their passwords are safe today but their encrypted archives may not be, and what FIPS 203/204/205 change; (d) submit the capstone assessment above. Passing means no hype survives your explanation.

Corrections

The hype, graded.

"Quantum computers try all answers at once." Misleading. A superposition does hold many computational paths, but measurement reveals only one sample from a probability distribution. Without interference choreography amplifying the right answer, parallelism buys nothing — which is why quantum speedups are rare and problem-specific, not general.

"Quantum computers will replace classical computers." False. Quantum processors accelerate specific structured problems (period-finding, unstructured search, quantum simulation); they are worse than classical machines at everything else and will remain coprocessors, not replacements, for the foreseeable future.

"Shor's algorithm means all encryption is already broken." False. Shor breaks RSA/ECC in principle on a fault-tolerant machine that does not exist; symmetric encryption (AES) and hashing are only quadratically weakened. The honest statement: public-key cryptography has a known expiration date, which is why NIST standardized replacements in 2024.

"More qubits = closer to breaking RSA." Misleading. A thousand noisy physical qubits are not one-thousandth of the way to a cryptographically relevant machine — without error correction, qubit count alone says almost nothing about capability. Logical (error-corrected) qubits are the metric that matters, and the industry is still counting those in single digits.

"Quantum supremacy proved quantum computers are useful." Contested. Google's 2019 Sycamore result demonstrated a task hard for classical supercomputers at that time — random-circuit sampling, a benchmark with no practical use — and subsequent classical-algorithm improvements narrowed the claimed gap. Supremacy on a useless task is a physics milestone, not a product.

"Entanglement enables instant communication." False. Entangled measurement outcomes are correlated, but the outcomes themselves are random, and no information can be transmitted faster than light — this is the no-communication theorem, and it is why quantum networks still need classical channels alongside.

"Post-quantum cryptography is unproven and can wait." Risky. ML-KEM and ML-DSA rest on lattice problems studied for decades, and "harvest now, decrypt later" makes waiting the expensive option for long-lived data. The migration takes years; NIST, NSA (CNSA 2.0), and major browsers have already started.
