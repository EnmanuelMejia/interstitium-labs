The sequence

What this sequence is, and how to work it.

What this is: a management-level study sequence for CISM — ISACA's Certified Information Security Manager credential. It covers the four domains of the security manager's craft: information security governance, information risk management, information security program development and management, and incident management. Every module is scenario-driven, because the credential tests judgment under realistic constraints, not vocabulary.

Who it is for: working security practitioners moving into management, IT leaders taking on security responsibility, and experienced professionals preparing for the CISM certification. The posture throughout is the manager's chair: you translate technical reality into business decisions, own the risk register, and answer to a board.

Prerequisites: familiarity with core security concepts (you should know what a firewall, a vulnerability scan, and an incident are); professional experience in information security is expected by ISACA for the certification itself — check the current experience requirements on the official ISACA page before planning your path. No single technology stack is assumed.

How to use it: work the modules in order and treat the checkpoints as the real exam — each is a scenario requiring a management decision, answered in writing. Do the labs as artifacts, not exercises: the charter excerpt, risk register, dashboard spec, and tabletop script you produce here become your capstone kit and, frankly, a portfolio. Keep ISACA's official certification pages open as your authoritative reference.

Outcomes

What you will be able to do.

  • Operate as a security manager, not a technician: translate security needs into business language that executives, boards, and auditors can act on.
  • Build and defend business cases for security investments using risk quantification, cost-benefit framing, and alignment to organizational strategy.
  • Design, implement, and govern an enterprise information security program end to end: strategy, policies, standards, controls, and oversight.
  • Lead risk assessment and treatment cycles, own the risk register, and report residual risk to decision-makers in terms they understand.
  • Direct incident response at the management level: define escalation criteria, run tabletops, manage communications, and convert lessons learned into program change.

The curriculum

7 modules, in study order.

Module 1

Governance Foundations: Culture, Strategy, and the Security Charter

Learning objectives

  • Explain why enterprise governance of information is a board-level duty and what a security manager owes senior leadership.
  • Draft a security charter excerpt that defines the information security function's authority, responsibilities, and reporting lines.
  • Align a security strategy with organizational mission, risk appetite, and stakeholder expectations.
  • Differentiate roles and accountability among the board, senior management, data owners, custodians, and the information security manager.

Key concepts

Enterprise governance of information as a board-level duty

Information security governance is not an IT activity that reports upward — it is an enterprise governance duty that starts at the board. The board sets risk appetite and holds management accountable; senior leadership owns the risk decisions. The security manager's job is to make those decisions informed ones.

This framing changes everything about how you communicate: you are not asking for toys, you are presenting the board with the risk information it needs to discharge its duty. Learn to speak in that register or the program stays unfunded.

Information security strategy as a business enabler

A security strategy that reads as a list of controls is a cost center; a strategy that traces to business objectives — market expansion, customer trust, regulatory standing — is an enabler. Every strategic goal should answer: which business outcome does this protect or unlock?

When the strategy enables, budget conversations change from 'why does security cost so much' to 'what risk are we accepting by not funding this.' That reframe is the security manager's most valuable political skill.

The security charter: scope, authority, responsibilities, independence

The security charter is the function's founding document: it states the purpose, the scope of authority (what the security function can direct, approve, or veto), its responsibilities, and — critically — its reporting line and independence. Without a charter, the security manager has opinions; with one, they have a mandate.

Independence is the point most often gutted: a security function buried under the CIO with no board access cannot objectively assess IT's own risks. The charter must guarantee the reporting line that preserves that independence, and you must be willing to defend it.

Roles and responsibilities: board, executives, owners, custodians

The board oversees; executives sponsor and resource; data owners (business leaders) decide classification and acceptable risk for their information; custodians (often IT) implement the controls; the information security manager advises, monitors, and reports. Confusing these roles is how accountability evaporates.

The owner-versus-custodian distinction does the most work: the business owner decides what the data is worth and what risk is acceptable; IT implements. When IT starts making risk-acceptance decisions, governance has failed — your job is to notice and correct it.

Policies as management instruments: the cascade

A policy is a management statement of intent — short, stable, approved at senior level. It cascades: policies into standards (mandatory rules), standards into baselines (minimum configurations), baselines into procedures (step-by-step how). Guidelines sit alongside as recommended practice.

The cascade matters because each level has a different approval authority and change cadence. Procedures change monthly; policies change yearly. If your 'policy' needs monthly edits, it is actually a procedure wearing a policy's clothes — and it will never get the senior attention a real policy commands.

Aligning security strategy to business strategy and risk appetite

Alignment is not a slogan — it is a traceability exercise: business objective to security objective to initiative to metric. If an initiative cannot be traced upward to something the business cares about, it does not belong in the strategy.

Risk appetite is the other anchor: the strategy must state plainly which risks the organization will carry and which it will not. A strategy without an appetite statement is a wish list; with one, it is a contract about what 'secure enough' means here.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Charter excerpt. Draft a one-page security charter excerpt: purpose, authority, responsibilities, and reporting line of the information security function for a fictional mid-size logistics company. Have a peer attack its independence provisions.
  2. Lab 2 — Strategy traceability. Write three executive-level security objectives that trace from a fictional company's strategic goals (market expansion, customer trust, regulatory compliance) to concrete security outcomes. Draw the traceability lines explicitly.
  3. Lab 3 — RACI matrix. Map a RACI matrix for security governance decisions — policy approval, risk acceptance, budget allocation, incident escalation — across board, CEO, CIO, CISO, and business-unit heads. Find the decision with the muddiest accountability and fix it.
  4. Lab 4 — Board memo. Draft a short briefing memo to a fictional board explaining why the security function's reporting line matters for independence and oversight. One page, no jargon, decision required at the end.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: The CEO wants the CISO to report to the CIO with no dotted line to the board. As the security manager, what governance risks do you raise and what alternative do you recommend?
  2. Scenario: A business unit launches a customer-facing app without a security review. What governance mechanisms should have caught this, and what management action do you take now?
  3. Self-test: List five elements that belong in a security charter and explain, for each, what management decision it enables.

Module 2

Governance in Practice: Metrics, Maturity, and Stakeholder Management

Learning objectives

  • Design a metrics dashboard specification that lets senior management evaluate the effectiveness of the security program.
  • Use maturity and capability models to assess the current state of a security program and plan its development.
  • Identify security stakeholders, their information needs, and a communication plan that keeps them engaged.
  • Apply governance framework concepts to structure oversight without becoming a compliance-only function.

Key concepts

Governance metrics: KPIs, KRIs, activity versus effectiveness

KPIs (key performance indicators) measure how the program is performing; KRIs (key risk indicators) warn that risk is rising. Patch compliance at 99% is an activity measure — it says work happened. Mean time to remediate criticals is an effectiveness measure — it says risk went down. The checkpoint's scenario is the classic trap: activity looks green while effectiveness decays.

Every metric on your dashboard must inform a management decision, or it is decoration. Before adding a metric, name the decision it changes; before removing one, name who will miss it.

Maturity models and capability assessment

A maturity model scores the program's capabilities on a leveled scale — from ad hoc to optimized — across domains like governance, risk, program management, and incident response. It turns 'how are we doing' from an opinion into a structured assessment with evidence.

Use maturity for planning, not boasting: the gap between current and target maturity per domain is your roadmap input. And never present a maturity score without the evidence behind it — an un-evidenced 'level 4' is marketing, and audit committees can tell.

Stakeholder identification, analysis, and management

The security function has stakeholders — the board, executives, business units, IT, auditors, regulators, customers — each with different interests, influence, and information needs. A stakeholder register records them and the communication approach for each.

This is not bureaucracy; it is how you stop being surprised. The business unit that launches apps without security review (Module 1's scenario) is a stakeholder you failed to engage early. Map them before they map you.

Governance frameworks as structuring tools

Governance frameworks give you a skeleton for oversight: control objectives organized by domain, with practices you can adopt rather than invent. They structure the program and give auditors a common language.

The caution from the curriculum: frameworks structure oversight; they do not substitute for judgment. A framework-compliant program that ignores its actual risks is the compliance-only failure mode — certified, and breached. Use the framework to organize; use risk to prioritize.

Management reporting: translating technical findings into risk language

Executives do not need vulnerability counts; they need to know which business risks changed, what decisions are required, and what happens if they decide nothing. Every technical finding in your report must end in a business consequence.

The quarterly report template lab forces the discipline: one page, posture, risks, incidents, metric trends, decisions needed. If a section does not drive a decision, cut it. Brevity is a governance control.

Continuous improvement through audit feedback and lessons learned

Governance is a loop, not a launch: audit findings, incident lessons, and metric trends feed back into strategy and program design. The organization that treats an audit as a pass/fail event learns nothing; the one that mines findings for systemic causes improves.

Close the loop visibly — report back what changed because of what was learned. Nothing builds board confidence like demonstrating that the program gets smarter every cycle.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Dashboard specification. Design a metrics dashboard spec: 6–8 metrics mixing KPI and KRI for a fictional retailer, each with definition, data source, reporting frequency, and the management decision it informs. Kill any metric that informs no decision.
  2. Lab 2 — Maturity self-assessment. Assess a fictional company's program against a five-level maturity scale across governance, risk, program, and incident domains; write a one-paragraph current-state summary per domain with evidence cited.
  3. Lab 3 — Stakeholder register. Build a stakeholder register for a security program rollout: at least six stakeholders with interests, influence, and a tailored communication approach for each.
  4. Lab 4 — Quarterly report template. Write an executive quarterly report template: one-page structure with posture, risks, incidents, metrics trend, and decisions needed. Then fill it with fictional data and time yourself presenting it in five minutes.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: Your dashboard shows patch compliance at 99% but mean time to remediate critical vulnerabilities is rising. What management questions do you ask, and which metric would you add?
  2. Scenario: The audit committee asks whether the security program is 'good enough.' How do you answer using maturity assessment and metrics rather than opinion?

Module 3

Risk Fundamentals: Identifying, Classifying, and Valuing Information Assets and Threats

Learning objectives

  • Establish a repeatable risk identification process covering assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and business impact.
  • Lead asset classification and ownership exercises that produce a defensible information asset inventory.
  • Evaluate emerging threat trends and translate them into risk scenarios relevant to a specific organization.
  • Distinguish inherent, control, and residual risk and explain their use in management decision-making.

Key concepts

Asset identification, classification, and ownership

Risk management starts with knowing what you have: an information asset inventory listing systems, data stores, and services, each with a named owner and a classification. Without the inventory, risk assessment is theater — you cannot assess what you have not identified.

Classification tiers (public, internal, confidential, restricted — labels vary) drive handling rules. The owner, a business leader, decides the classification; the security manager facilitates an honest exercise, not a rubber stamp.

Threat identification: sources, actors, emerging technology

Threats are the 'who and what' that could harm assets: external attackers, insiders, partners, natural events — and now AI-enabled phishing, supply-chain compromise, and whatever this year's emerging technology brings. Identify them per asset, not in the abstract.

The emerging-threats briefing lab builds the executive skill: translating a threat trend into business terms for your specific organization. 'Ransomware is rising' is noise; 'our backup restoration has never been tested and ransomware dwell time now exceeds our detection window' is a decision.

Vulnerability identification and technical assessment inputs

Vulnerabilities are the weaknesses threats exploit — unpatched systems, misconfigurations, weak processes. Technical assessments (scans, pen tests, audits) produce the raw findings; the manager's job is to feed them into the risk process rather than file them.

A vulnerability without a threat and an asset is just a finding. The risk chain — asset, threat, vulnerability, impact — is what turns scan output into management information.

Business impact analysis: linking compromise to consequences

BIA asks: if this asset were compromised, what happens to the business? Operational disruption, financial loss, regulatory penalty, reputational damage — quantified or at least ordered. It is the bridge between technical risk and executive concern.

The BIA is also where criticality ranking comes from: not every system deserves the same protection spend. Impact analysis is how you defend differentiated investment to people who think security should cost the same everywhere.

Inherent risk, control effectiveness, residual risk

Inherent risk is the risk before controls — the raw exposure. Controls reduce it, and their effectiveness determines how much. Residual risk is what remains after controls, and it is the only number that matters to decision-makers.

The 'never been breached' fallacy from the scenario dies here: absence of incidents is not evidence of low inherent risk, it is evidence about the past. Reframe around inherent versus residual, and the conversation becomes about controls, not luck.

Risk scenarios as management communication tools

A risk scenario narrates asset, threat actor, vulnerability, and business consequence in a paragraph an executive can picture. Scenarios are the unit of risk communication — registers store them, dashboards summarize them, board memos lead with them.

Write scenarios concretely: named actor, plausible path, quantified consequence. 'A phishing compromise of a finance workstation leads to a fraudulent wire' beats 'email-borne threats pose medium-high risk' in every room that matters.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Asset inventory. Build an information asset inventory for a fictional healthcare SaaS startup: 10+ assets with classification tiers, owners, and a one-line business-impact rationale for each.
  2. Lab 2 — Risk scenarios. Write three risk scenarios (threat actor, vulnerability, business consequence) for a fictional financial services firm adopting cloud infrastructure. Make each one briefable in sixty seconds.
  3. Lab 3 — Emerging-threats briefing. Draft a briefing note for senior management: two current threat trends, each explained in business terms with a clear 'why this matters to us' and a recommended next step.
  4. Lab 4 — Risk chain map. Map a sample risk chain from asset through threat and vulnerability to business impact, labeling inherent risk, applied controls, and residual risk at each stage.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: Your board will fund exactly one initiative this quarter: a $60k endpoint-protection upgrade OR a $60k vendor-risk-management program. The company has suffered one phishing compromise (contained) and is about to sign three new SaaS vendors. Write a half-page recommendation naming the initiative you fund, the risk you are accepting by defunding the other, and how you would measure success in 90 days.
  2. Scenario: Two business units classify the same shared database differently. What management process resolves the conflict, and who owns the decision?

Module 4

Risk Assessment and Treatment: The Risk Register and the Business Case

Learning objectives

  • Run a risk assessment end to end: scope, methodology selection, analysis, and documentation of results.
  • Build and maintain a risk register that executives can read and act on.
  • Recommend and justify risk treatment options: mitigate, transfer, accept, or avoid.
  • Construct a business case for a security investment using cost-benefit and risk-reduction framing.

Key concepts

Risk assessment methodologies: qualitative, quantitative, semi-quantitative

Qualitative assessment uses ordered scales (high/medium/low) — fast, workshop-friendly, and sufficient when precision would be false precision. Quantitative puts dollar values on likelihood and impact — powerful for business cases, but only as good as its data. Semi-quantitative blends them with numbered scales.

Choose the methodology the decision needs, not the one that impresses. A simple matrix beats a dollar model when the inputs are guesses; a dollar model beats a matrix when the CFO is deciding on a $250K tool. Match the rigor to the stakes.

Risk analysis: likelihood, impact, control evaluation

Analysis estimates two things — how likely, how bad — and then evaluates existing controls to derive residual risk. The control evaluation step is where assessments most often fail: controls assumed effective but never tested produce residual-risk numbers that are fiction.

Test the controls you are counting on, or discount them. A firewall nobody reviews rules for is not a control; it is a hope with a purchase order.

Risk treatment and the acceptance process

Four treatments: mitigate (add controls), transfer (insurance, outsourcing — note it transfers financial impact, rarely the accountability), accept (documented, by someone with authority, with a review date), avoid (stop the activity). Acceptance is a management decision, not a default — undocumented acceptance is just neglect.

The nine-month-fix scenario is the real world: you cannot mitigate in time, so you document interim acceptance with compensating controls, a named accepting authority, and a review date. Risk accepted explicitly is governance; risk accepted by silence is an incident waiting for a date.

The risk register: structure, ownership, lifecycle

The risk register is the central management artifact: each risk with description, likelihood, impact, inherent and residual ratings, controls, treatment decision, owner, and review date. It is a living document — risks age, treatments complete, new risks arrive.

Ownership is per-risk and named: a risk owned by 'the team' is owned by no one. Review cadence keeps the register honest; a register untouched for three years (the CISSP Module 1 scenario) is an archaeological artifact, not a management tool.

Cost-benefit analysis and return on security investment

A business case frames security spend as risk reduction per dollar: what the investment costs, what loss expectancy it reduces, what alternatives were considered, and what residual risk remains. It does not promise 'no breaches' — it promises a better risk position for the money.

The CFO's 'nothing bad has happened' is answered with expected value, not fear: the cost of the control versus the annualized cost of the risk, in the language of budgets. Security managers who cannot build this case do not get funded.

Integrating risk assessment into project, procurement, and change decisions

Risk assessment cannot be an annual ritual — it must inject into the decisions where risk is actually created: new projects, vendor procurement, and production changes. The Module 1 scenario (an app launched without security review) is what happens when it does not.

Integration means process hooks with teeth: no production launch without a risk review, no vendor contract without security clauses, no major change without a risk note. Build the hooks into other people's processes and risk management becomes ambient.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Risk register. Build a risk register for a fictional e-commerce company: 8–10 risks with description, likelihood, impact, inherent risk, existing controls, residual risk, treatment decision, and named owner.
  2. Lab 2 — Treatment tabletop. Take one high residual risk from your register and walk through all four treatment options; write a one-page recommendation memo with pros, cons, and cost framing for each.
  3. Lab 3 — Business case. Write a business case for a privileged access management tool: problem statement, risk reduction, cost estimate, alternatives considered, expected outcomes — all in executive language, two pages max.
  4. Lab 4 — Risk acceptance memo. Draft a risk acceptance memo template: risk description, business justification, compensating controls, review date, and signature block for the accepting authority.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: The CFO asks whether a $250K security tool is worth it when 'nothing bad has happened.' Build the management argument using risk treatment and cost-of-breach concepts.
  2. Scenario: A critical risk exceeds the organization's risk appetite but the fix takes nine months. What treatment path do you recommend and how do you document interim acceptance?

Module 5

Building the Program: Strategy, Resources, Standards, and Controls

Learning objectives

  • Develop an information security program strategy that operationalizes the security strategy into funded initiatives.
  • Plan program resources: people, budget, and sourcing models (in-house, outsourced, hybrid).
  • Design the program's control environment: policies, standards, baselines, and procedures that cascade coherently.
  • Establish program management practices: roadmaps, milestones, and stakeholder reporting for multi-year initiatives.

Key concepts

Program strategy and its relationship to enterprise strategy

The program strategy operationalizes the security strategy: it turns 'protect customer trust' into funded initiatives with owners, timelines, and success criteria. If the security strategy is the what and why, the program strategy is the how, when, and with what money.

It must also survive contact with budget reality — the 40%-cut scenario is normal, not exceptional. Reprioritization under constraint, with explicit residual-risk communication, is the skill being tested.

Resource planning: staffing, budgeting, make-vs-buy

Staffing models range from fully in-house to fully outsourced to hybrid; the make-vs-buy decision weighs control, expertise, cost, and speed. A managed detection service versus two hires is decided on coverage hours, skill depth, institutional knowledge, and exit cost — not on sticker price.

Budget the program in categories (people, tools, services, training) across the roadmap horizon, with assumptions written down. Assumptions are what let you replan intelligently when the budget moves.

Security architecture at the management level

The manager does not design firewalls — but must ensure the architecture aligns controls to business processes and that someone is accountable for the whole picture. Architecture review is a governance checkpoint: does this design implement our policies, and who signed off that it does?

Your lever is the review gate, not the CLI. Require architecture review for significant changes, staff it with people who understand both the business process and the threat model, and track its findings like any other risk input.

The policy hierarchy: policies, standards, baselines, guidelines, procedures

Policy states management intent (approved at senior level, changes slowly). Standards set mandatory rules. Baselines define minimum secure configurations. Procedures give step-by-step instructions. Guidelines recommend. Each level has its own author, approver, and review cycle.

The lab's hierarchy chart makes this concrete: one policy statement expanded into three standards with baselines and procedures beneath. If you cannot draw the cascade for your program, you do not have a program — you have documents.

Control selection frameworks and control objectives

Control frameworks organize the universe of possible controls by domain and objective, giving you a menu to select from rather than a blank page. Selection is risk-driven: the risk assessment says what needs controlling, the framework suggests how.

Control objectives — what the control must achieve — matter more than control names. Two organizations can satisfy the same objective with different controls; auditors test the objective, so design to it.

Roadmaps, milestones, and benefits tracking

A roadmap sequences initiatives across phases with dependencies and success criteria per phase. Milestones mark checkable progress; benefits tracking verifies the program delivered the risk reduction it promised.

The roadmap is also your reprioritization tool when budgets cut: with dependencies mapped, you can show exactly which outcomes slip when funding moves, turning 'do more with less' into an explicit residual-risk conversation.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Three-year roadmap. Draft a three-year security program roadmap for a fictional manufacturing firm: phases, major initiatives per phase, dependencies, and success criteria for each phase.
  2. Lab 2 — Staffing plan. Write a staffing plan for a small security team: roles, reporting lines, and which functions to outsource versus build in-house, with a written rationale for each sourcing decision.
  3. Lab 3 — Policy hierarchy chart. Expand one information security policy statement into three standards with the baselines and procedures beneath them. Name the approver and review cycle for each level.
  4. Lab 4 — Program budget outline. Build a budget outline across people, tools, services, and training, allocated over the three-year roadmap, with the assumptions behind each number stated explicitly.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: The board approved strategy but cut the requested budget by 40%. As security manager, how do you reprioritize the roadmap and communicate the resulting residual risk?
  2. Scenario: A vendor proposes a managed detection service to replace two planned hires. What management criteria guide your sourcing recommendation?

Module 6

Running the Program: Awareness, Operations, and Assurance

Learning objectives

  • Design a security awareness and training program with measurable behavior outcomes, not just completion rates.
  • Establish operational management practices for the program: change, configuration, and third-party considerations.
  • Use audit, assessment, and testing results as program inputs that drive prioritized improvement.
  • Manage program performance through continuous monitoring and periodic review cycles.

Key concepts

Security awareness: design, delivery, measuring behavior change

Awareness programs exist to change behavior, not to generate completion certificates. Design segments audiences (executives, developers, finance staff face different threats), delivers through multiple channels, and measures outcomes — phishing click rates, reporting rates, incident causes — not attendance.

Flat click rates after two quarters mean the training is not working, not that users are untrainable. The management response is to change the program: different content, different frequency, simulated scenarios matched to real attacks — and to ask whether the underlying control (email filtering, MFA) is carrying weight the training cannot.

Training for specialized roles versus general awareness

General workforce awareness covers phishing, passwords, and reporting. Specialized roles need specialized training: developers need secure coding, finance staff need fraud recognition, executives need targeted-threat briefings, incident responders need tabletops.

One-size training wastes everyone's time and teaches nothing well. Segment, specialize, and make the specialized training hands-on — a developer who has exploited a lab vulnerability remembers it; one who clicked through slides does not.

Operational security management: change and configuration

Security must be embedded in change management and configuration management — the processes that already govern production changes. Every significant change gets a security review; configurations have secure baselines and drift is detected.

The third-year repeat audit finding is the symptom of security bolted on instead of built in: the control fails because no operational process sustains it. The structural fix is to move the control into the change pipeline so compliance becomes automatic.

Third-party and supply-chain risk

Vendors and suppliers extend your attack surface under someone else's management. The program needs a third-party risk process: security review before procurement, contract clauses (right to audit, breach notification, data return/destruction), and periodic reassessment.

You cannot outsource accountability — a vendor breach is your breach in the eyes of customers and regulators. The ten-question pre-signature checklist from the lab is the minimum viable version of this process.

Assurance activities: audits, assessments, pen tests, control testing

Audits test compliance, assessments test design, penetration tests test resistance, control testing tests operation — four different questions, four different methods. The manager's job is to run the right assurance at the right cadence and to feed every result back into the program.

Treat assurance as input, not judgment: a pen test finding is a free lesson about your real exposure. Programs that dread audits hide; programs that mine them improve. Be the second kind.

Program performance monitoring and continuous improvement

The program needs its own performance loop: metrics reviewed on cadence, root-cause analysis on repeat findings, and structural changes when patterns emerge. The quarterly program review (standing agenda, data inputs, named attendees, decisions per item) is the mechanism.

Continuous improvement is the difference between a program and a project. Projects end; programs adapt. Build the review cadence into the calendar now, or urgency will crowd it out forever.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — 12-month awareness program. Design a 12-month awareness program for a fictional insurance company: audience segments, topics per quarter, delivery channels, and two behavior-based success measures. No completion-rate metrics allowed.
  2. Lab 2 — Audit response plan. Write a management response plan template for audit findings: finding summary, root cause, remediation owner, timeline, and verification step. Apply it to a fictional repeat finding.
  3. Lab 3 — Vendor security checklist. Draft a third-party security review checklist for onboarding a new SaaS vendor: ten questions a security manager asks before contract signature, plus the contract clauses required.
  4. Lab 4 — Quarterly review agenda. Build a quarterly program review agenda: standing items, data inputs, attendees, and the specific decision each item is meant to produce.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: Phishing simulation click rates stay flat after two quarters of training. What management conclusions do you draw and what program changes do you propose?
  2. Scenario: An audit finds the same control deficiency for the third year running. What does this tell you about program management, and what do you change structurally?

Module 7

Incident Management: Preparation, Response Leadership, and Recovery

Learning objectives

  • Design an incident management program: plans, roles, escalation criteria, and communication channels.
  • Lead incident response at the management level: triage, escalation, stakeholder communication, and decision-making under pressure.
  • Run a tabletop incident exercise that tests plans, people, and decisions rather than tools.
  • Convert post-incident lessons into program changes: root-cause follow-through and control improvement.

Key concepts

Incident management program: plans, teams, roles

The incident management program defines the plan, the response team, and everyone's role before anything happens — because the middle of an incident is the worst time to decide who decides. Roles cover technical response, communications, legal liaison, and executive decision-making.

The program is tested, not just written: tabletops exercise the people and the decisions, not the tools. An untested plan is a draft with delusions of grandeur.

Classification, prioritization, and escalation criteria

Incidents are classified by severity with clear criteria, and each level triggers defined escalation: who is notified, who has decision authority, target response times. The escalation matrix from the lab is the artifact — one page, unambiguous, posted where it will be found at 3 AM.

Without criteria, every incident is either under- or over-escalated. The matrix removes judgment from the notification decision so judgment can be spent on the response.

Business continuity and disaster recovery interfaces

Incident management handles the event; business continuity keeps the business running through it; disaster recovery restores IT services after it. The three plans must interface cleanly — handoff criteria, shared roles, consistent severity language — or they collide mid-crisis.

The untested-backup scenario is the classic interface failure: DR assumed backups worked, nobody verified. Test restoration on schedule and report the result upward as a program metric, not a technical detail.

Crisis communication: internal, external, regulatory, media

A breach creates four communication fronts: internal (staff need instructions, not rumors), customers (notification with legal review), regulators (mandatory timelines in many jurisdictions), and media (one trained spokesperson, never the engineer). The legal-versus-PR tension from the scenario is resolved by process: who owns the decision, what the notification obligations require, and a pre-agreed holding statement.

Prepare the communication templates and the decision tree before you need them. During the incident, communication discipline — single voice, verified facts, no speculation — protects the organization as much as any technical containment.

Forensics and evidence handling from a management perspective

The manager does not image disks but must ensure the team preserves evidence correctly: order of volatility (memory before disk, volatile before persistent), chain of custody documentation, and legal hold coordination. Mishandled evidence is inadmissible and sometimes a liability.

Your management actions: make sure forensic capability (internal or retained) is contracted before the incident, that responders know the preservation priorities, and that legal is involved early when evidence may matter.

Post-incident review and lessons-learned integration

Every significant incident ends with a blameless post-incident review: timeline, what worked, what failed, root causes, corrective actions with owners and deadlines. The review's output feeds directly into program changes — control improvements, plan updates, training topics.

The loop must close visibly: track corrective actions to completion and report them. An organization that writes lessons down and changes nothing has performed theater; the manager's job is to make the learning stick.

Hands-on labs

  1. Lab 1 — Tabletop exercise. Write a ransomware scenario for a fictional regional bank: facilitator script with five injects, discussion questions per inject, and a scoring rubric for the debrief. Run it with anyone who will sit still.
  2. Lab 2 — Incident report template. Write an incident report template: timeline, impact assessment, containment and eradication actions, communications log, root cause, and corrective actions with owners.
  3. Lab 3 — Escalation matrix. Draft an escalation matrix: severity levels, notification list per level, decision authority, and target response times. Keep it to one page.
  4. Lab 4 — Lessons-learned action plan. Write a post-incident action plan from three fictional findings, each with corrective action, owner, deadline, and the program change it drives.

Self-check questions

  1. Scenario: During a suspected data breach, legal wants silence while PR wants a proactive statement. As the security manager leading response, how do you structure the decision and who owns it?
  2. Scenario: The tabletop reveals the backup restoration procedure has never been tested. What management actions follow, and how do you report this gap upward?

Capstone

Prove it: the capstone project.

The capstone puts you in the chair: you are the newly hired information security manager of a fictional 500-employee financial services company with no formal security program. Deliver a management-ready starter kit: (1) a one-page security charter excerpt, (2) an information asset inventory with classifications, (3) a risk register of at least 10 risks with treatment recommendations, (4) a business case for the single highest-priority investment, (5) a metrics dashboard specification, (6) an incident escalation matrix plus a tabletop exercise script, and (7) a 12-month awareness program outline.

Present the kit as an executive briefing package with a cover memo explaining your sequencing, your resource needs, and the top three decisions leadership must make in the first 90 days. Every module in this sequence built one piece of this kit — the capstone is where you prove the pieces fit together and that you can defend them to people who control budgets.
