The course

What this course is, and how to work it.

What this is: a sixteen-lesson study course on applying artificial intelligence to cybersecurity — both directions of the relationship. It teaches how machine learning aids the security practitioner (threat detection, anomaly hunting, UEBA, intelligence analysis, network and cloud defense) and how the practitioner must defend the AI systems themselves (adversarial attacks, hardening, agentic-AI risk, governance). The lessons are designed to be worked in order: each builds on the data, modeling, and operational foundations laid earlier.

Who it is for: security analysts, SOC operators, incident responders, and engineers who want to understand where machine learning genuinely earns its place in security operations — and where it does not. Some familiarity with basic security concepts and a willingness to work with data are assumed; the ML foundations lesson exists precisely so that no prior machine-learning background is required.

How to use it: read each lesson overview below as the conceptual scaffold, then work the matching material in the interactive bootcamp, where the ideas become hands-on: adaptive scenarios, labs, and a simulated environment. Finish with the capstone integration lesson, which asks you to fuse the whole arc into one end-to-end defensive posture.

The curriculum

Sixteen lessons, in study order.

Lesson 1

AI & Cybersecurity in the Agentic Era

Security work has always been an asymmetric contest of cognition: attackers need one working path, defenders must hold all of them. This lesson frames the modern landscape — defenders now automate detection and triage with machine learning while attackers automate reconnaissance, phishing, and malware mutation with the same toolkits. The practitioner’s job is to understand this symmetry and pick their leverage points deliberately: where AI genuinely multiplies human judgment (correlating high-volume telemetry, prioritizing alerts, drafting response scaffolding) and where human judgment must remain authoritative (scope decisions, containment calls, communicating with the business). The agentic era adds a third consideration: AI agents that act on their own — triage bots, remediation scripts, autonomous scanners — become part of the attack surface and part of the defense, simultaneously.

  • Defender/attacker asymmetry
  • Human-in-the-loop design
  • Agentic systems as attack surface

Lesson 2

ML Foundations for Security

Before touching a security dataset, you need a working mental model of how learning algorithms behave: training versus inference, supervised versus unsupervised setups, features and labels, precision versus recall, and the difference between a model that generalizes and one that memorized the training set. Security data punishes sloppy foundations harder than most domains — adversaries actively shift distributions, labeled attack data is rare and biased, and false positives burn analyst trust at a rate no accuracy score captures. This lesson builds the vocabulary every later lesson leans on: overfitting and concept drift as security problems, not textbook curiosities, and evaluation metrics translated into operational terms — what does a 1% false-positive rate mean when it multiplies across ten thousand endpoints a day?

  • Supervised vs. unsupervised
  • Precision/recall in SOC terms
  • Overfitting & concept drift

Lesson 3

Data Engineering for Security ML

Every security ML effort lives or dies on its data plumbing, and security data is some of the ugliest in any industry: heterogeneous log formats, inconsistent timestamps, missing fields, volumes that make laptops weep, and labeling that is often an analyst’s best guess retroactively applied. This lesson covers the pipeline that turns raw telemetry — endpoint logs, NetFlow, authentication events, DNS, email metadata — into model-ready datasets: collection and normalization, time-windowing and aggregation, handling class imbalance (attacks are rare by definition), and building labeling workflows that don’t silently poison the training set with the SOC’s own blind spots. The discipline here is unglamorous and decisive: a mediocre model on honest data outperforms a sophisticated model on garbage every time.

  • Log normalization
  • Class imbalance
  • Labeling workflows

Lesson 4

Supervised Threat Detection

When you have labeled examples of malicious behavior — known malware families, confirmed phishing campaigns, categorized intrusion attempts — supervised learning can turn past detections into classifiers that flag the future. This lesson covers the practitioner’s workflow: framing a detection problem as a classification task, choosing model families suited to the data (tree ensembles for tabular telemetry, sequences for command lines and DNS strings), tuning for the operational sweet spot between detection rate and alert volume, and validating on data the model has never seen. It also confronts the central limitation honestly: supervised models detect variations of what they’ve been shown. They are memory, not imagination — essential for known threats, blind to the genuinely novel.

  • Classification for detection
  • Model selection for telemetry
  • Known-threat limits

Lesson 5

Anomaly Detection & Unsupervised Learning

The novel threats that supervised models miss demand a different strategy: learn what normal looks like, and flag what deviates. This lesson covers the unsupervised toolkit — clustering, density estimation, autoencoders, isolation forests — and the operational reality of deploying it: baselines drift with every business change, an anomaly is not an attack until an analyst confirms it, and the tuning dial between sensitivity and alert fatigue is the hardest dial in the SOC. Done well, anomaly detection surfaces lateral movement, insider deviation, and misconfigurations that signature logic never considered. Done poorly, it becomes a noise generator that analysts learn to ignore — which is worse than having no detector at all.

  • Baseline modeling
  • Clustering & autoencoders
  • Alert-fatigue economics

Lesson 6

Endpoint Detection: EDR Literacy

Endpoint detection and response platforms are where most analysts first meet security ML in production — behavioral engines, cloud reputation scoring, and ML-driven verdicts already embedded in the tools they use daily. This lesson builds EDR literacy from the operator’s chair: how detection pipelines combine signatures, heuristics, and models; how to read a detection with its confidence and provenance attached; how false positives propagate through containment actions; and how to work with EDR telemetry in investigations rather than treating it as a black-box oracle. The goal is not to replace the vendor tool but to be its competent master — understanding enough of the model’s reasoning to trust, verify, and override it.

  • Detection pipelines
  • Verdict interpretation
  • Human override discipline

Lesson 7

Feature Engineering & Visualization

The most powerful security models on record were won not by better algorithms but by better features — the crafted signals that encode analyst intuition in machine-readable form: entropy measures on domains, temporal patterns in authentication bursts, graph features across process trees. This lesson covers feature engineering as a security craft: extracting behavioral signals from raw logs, transforming them so models can use them, and then closing the loop with visualization — because a feature the analyst cannot inspect is a detection the SOC cannot defend. Visualization is treated here as operational infrastructure, not reporting decoration: dashboards and plots that let a human sanity-check what the model is actually reacting to.

  • Behavioral features
  • Domain signal craft
  • Explainable inspection

Lesson 8

Vulnerability Assessment & Incident Response

AI enters vulnerability management as a prioritization engine — scoring and clustering vulnerabilities by exploitability and business context instead of raw CVSS — and enters incident response as a force multiplier for triage, enrichment, and containment planning. This lesson covers both sides with operational honesty: how predictive prioritization cuts the scan-to-fix queue down to what matters, how ML-assisted triage accelerates the first fifteen minutes of a response, and where the boundaries sit — AI drafts the playbook, the human owns the containment decision. It also addresses the feedback loop: every incident the AI helps resolve generates the labeled data that trains the next round of models.

  • Vulnerability prioritization
  • Triage acceleration
  • Containment authority

Lesson 9

UEBA — User and Entity Behavior Analytics

Insider threats and compromised accounts are the hardest detections in security because the activity looks almost legitimate — the credentials are real, the access patterns nearly normal. UEBA attacks this problem with per-entity baselines: modeling each user, service account, and device as its own behavioral profile and flagging deviations in context. This lesson covers how UEBA systems build and maintain those profiles, how risk scores compound across multiple weak signals, and the practical frictions: privacy and employee-trust concerns, the onboarding period before profiles are trustworthy, and the tuning required to keep the system from criminalizing ordinary workflow changes. It’s a lesson in calibrated suspicion — the mathematics of watching people without losing the people.

  • Behavioral baselines
  • Risk-score compounding
  • Insider-threat ethics

Lesson 10

NLP for Security Intelligence

A huge fraction of security work is text: threat reports, phishing lures, dark-web chatter, vendor advisories, internal tickets, attacker command histories. This lesson covers how natural language processing turns that text into operational intelligence — extracting indicators and TTPs from reports, classifying and clustering phishing campaigns, summarizing long incident timelines, and mining open-source intelligence at machine speed. It also warns where NLP fails in security: jargon-heavy attacker slang that breaks generic language models, adversarial text crafted to evade classifiers, and the hallucination risk when an analyst treats generated summaries as ground truth. The skill taught here is reading machine-produced intelligence with a source-criticism mindset.

  • Indicator extraction
  • Phishing classification
  • Intel summarization

Lesson 11

AI for Network & Cloud Security

Networks and clouds generate the highest-volume telemetry a security team handles — flows, DNS, API calls, identity events — and the lowest signal-to-noise ratio. This lesson covers applying ML at that scale: network anomaly detection on flow and DNS data, detecting command-and-control beacons and data exfiltration in traffic patterns, cloud misconfiguration and anomaly detection across multi-account estates, and the identity-layer models that flag impossible travel and token abuse. The operational thread is scale discipline: models that work in a lab fail in production when feature extraction can’t keep pace with ingest, so the lesson teaches performance-aware design — streaming features, sampling strategies, and where to place the models so they see enough without drowning.

  • Flow & DNS analytics
  • C2/beacon detection
  • Cloud & identity anomalies

Lesson 12

Adversarial AI: Attacks on ML Systems

The course turns here: every defensive model is itself software under attack, and attackers have adapted. This lesson catalogs the attack taxonomy — evasion (perturbing inputs so the model misclassifies, from adversarial malware samples to tricked phishing classifiers), poisoning (corrupting training data or feedback loops), extraction (stealing a model through its API), and inversion (recovering sensitive training data from model outputs). Each is taught with the practitioner’s question: how would this attack show up in your environment, and what would you see? A detection pipeline that silently degrades under evasion is a silent failure — this lesson is about making model compromise visible, monitored, and incident-ready.

  • Evasion & poisoning
  • Model extraction/inversion
  • Attack indicators

Lesson 13

Defending AI Systems & Adversarial Hardening

Cataloging attacks is half the lesson; hardening against them is the practitioner’s deliverable. This lesson covers the defensive repertoire: adversarial training and robust feature design, input validation and sanitization at model boundaries, monitoring models for drift that signals poisoning or evasion campaigns, securing the ML supply chain (datasets, pretrained weights, dependencies), and access controls that limit extraction and inversion. It also introduces the organizational muscle most teams lack — red-teaming your own models the way you pen-test your own networks, on a cadence, with findings tracked like vulnerabilities. A hardened model is not an invulnerable model; it’s one whose failure modes you’ve mapped and instrumented.

  • Adversarial training
  • Drift monitoring
  • ML red-teaming

Lesson 14

Agentic AI: Architecture, Automation & Security

AI agents — systems that plan, call tools, and act toward goals — are arriving in security operations as triage copilots, autonomous scanners, and remediation runners, and they are arriving in adversaries’ hands as autonomous reconnaissance and attack automation. This lesson covers the architecture (planners, tool-use, memory, guardrails), the security uses that actually work (investigative copilots, evidence gathering, playbook execution under human approval), and the risks unique to agency: prompt injection as the new injection vulnerability, agents exceeding their intended authority, compromised tool outputs poisoning the agent’s reasoning, and cascading autonomous actions with no human checkpoint. The practitioner’s framework here is privilege and containment for agents — the same least-privilege discipline applied to humans, now applied to software that acts.

  • Agent architectures
  • Prompt injection
  • Least-privilege agents

Lesson 15

Governance, Ethics & Responsible AI

Powerful security tooling deployed without governance becomes liability: biased models that profile employees unfairly, surveillance creep from UEBA rollouts, automated containment that punishes the innocent, and AI-assisted decisions with no audit trail when the board asks why. This lesson covers the governance layer — model documentation and risk registers for AI systems, bias testing on security datasets, human-oversight requirements for high-impact automated actions, data-handling rules for training on employee and customer data, and alignment with the regulatory landscape as it evolves. The practitioner framing: governance isn’t bureaucracy added after the engineering; it’s the design constraint that keeps your AI program defensible when it’s tested — by an auditor, a regulator, or your own workforce.

  • Model risk registers
  • Bias & surveillance limits
  • Auditability

Lesson 16

Capstone Integration

The course closes by fusing the arc: you design an end-to-end AI-aided security posture for a realistic organization — the data pipelines, the detection models and their evaluation story, the UEBA and NLP components, the adversarial hardening and monitoring of your own models, the agentic systems with their guardrails, and the governance register that keeps the whole thing accountable. The capstone’s value isn’t a grade; it’s the artifact — a reference architecture and operating plan you could defend to a CISO, with every component traceable to a lesson in this course and every limitation stated plainly. AI-aided cybersecurity, done right, is a program: models you understand, data you trust, adversaries you respect, and humans who remain in charge.

  • Reference architecture
  • Operating plan
  • Portfolio artifact
