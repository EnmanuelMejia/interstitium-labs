Edward Bernays: the engineering of consent.

The track

Why study the man who named the game.

Edward Bernays (1891–1995) did not invent public relations — Ivy Lee was practicing before him — but he gave it a theory, a professional title ("public relations counsel"), university courses, and a shelf of books that described the machinery out loud. His core claim, refined across Crystallizing Public Opinion (1923), Propaganda (1928), and the 1947 essay "The Engineering of Consent," was that in a mass society, public opinion is not something leaders discover; it is something trained specialists manufacture, using the social sciences, and that democracy cannot function without this work being done deliberately.

This track treats Bernays the way he treated his own subjects: as a case study in how narratives are built. Every campaign is reconstructed from verifiable records, every famous story is checked against its sources, and the myths — including several Bernays himself spread — are marked as such. If you finish this track, you will be able to do three things: explain his actual techniques in precise terms, separate documented history from PR folklore, and recognize the same techniques running in the media around you today.

A note on method. Bernays was a relentless self-mythologizer. His autobiography, his interviews, and even his books routinely inflate his role. Where a claim rests only on Bernays's own telling, this track says so. That discipline is the point: the engineering of consent begins with the engineering of Edward Bernays.

Chronology

A life in the manufacture of opinion.

Every date below is verifiable from published sources; the source list at the foot of this page names them.

  1. 1891

    November 22 — Edward Louis Bernays is born in Vienna, Austria-Hungary, to Jewish parents. His mother is Anna Freud, Sigmund Freud's sister; his father Eli Bernays is the brother of Freud's wife Martha — making Bernays Freud's nephew twice over.

  2. 1892

    The family emigrates to New York City, where Bernays grows up and attends DeWitt Clinton High School.

  3. 1912

    Graduates from Cornell University with a degree in agriculture — then chooses journalism and publicity instead.

  4. 1913–17

    First publicity work: theatrical producers, the Metropolitan Opera, and — while at the Medical Review of Reviews — publicity for the syphilis play Damaged Goods.

  5. 1917–18

    Serves on the U.S. Committee on Public Information (the Creel Committee): officially head of the Export Section and co-head of the Latin American Section of the Foreign Press Bureau. He travels with the committee to the Versailles Peace Conference in 1919. (Caution: documentation of his specific day-to-day duties is thin; his later accounts inflate the role.)

  6. 1919

    June — Opens his own public-relations office in New York with his wife, styling himself "public relations counsel."

  7. 1920

    Handles publicity for the NAACP's convention in Atlanta — its first held outside a northern city. He later receives an NAACP award.

  8. 1922

    Marries Doris E. Fleischman, his equal business partner and the co-author of much of the firm's work.

  9. 1923

    Publishes Crystallizing Public Opinion and teaches the first university public-relations course at NYU.

  10. 1924

    October 17 — Stages a "pancake breakfast" with vaudevillians for President Calvin Coolidge: one of the first overt publicity stunts built around a sitting president.

  11. 1928

    Publishes Propaganda; begins the Lucky Strike women's-smoking work for American Tobacco, including the "Reach for a Lucky instead of a sweet" line.

  12. 1929

    March 31 — Torches of Freedom: on Easter Sunday, at the Fifth Avenue Easter Parade, young women light Lucky Strikes as a staged gesture of emancipation. The New York Times reports it on April 1 under the headline "Group of Girls Puff at Cigarettes as a Gesture of 'Freedom.'"

  13. 1929

    October — Light's Golden Jubilee: Bernays directs national publicity for the 50th anniversary of Edison's light bulb, for General Electric and Henry Ford — special postage stamp, Edison's re-enactment broadcast by radio, culminating October 21 at Dearborn with Hoover, Ford, Orville Wright, Madame Curie, and John D. Rockefeller Jr.

  14. 1920s–30s

    Procter & Gamble's Ivory Soap (soap-sculpture contests; the floating contests), the Dixie Cup sanitation campaign, the Venida hairnet campaign.

  15. 1939

    Publicity director of the New York World's Fair.

  16. 1947

    March — "The Engineering of Consent" appears in the Annals of the American Academy of Political and Social Science, No. 250 — the essay that names the doctrine.

  17. 1950–54

    The United Fruit Company engagement: an anti-Arbenz PR blitz — a Guatemala Newsletter sent to 250 journalists, sponsored journalist tours of Guatemala, a Report on Guatemala distributed to Congress — framing Jacobo Árbenz's government as communist. During the June 1954 CIA coup (Operation PBSuccess), Bernays becomes a primary supplier of information to the AP/UPI/INS newswires. United Fruit drops him in 1959.

  18. 1955

    The Engineering of Consent published as an edited book (University of Oklahoma Press).

  19. 1960s

    In semi-retirement, works with anti-smoking lawyer John Banzhaf's group ASH — after decades of cigarette work.

  20. 1965

    Autobiography Biography of an Idea: Memoirs of Public Relations Counsel published.

  21. 1990

    Life magazine names him one of the 100 most influential Americans of the 20th century.

  22. 1995

    March 9 — Dies in Cambridge, Massachusetts, aged 103. The New York Times obituary (March 10) calls him "'Father of Public Relations' and Leader in Opinion Making."

Dramatis personae

The people around the machine.

01

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939)

The "double uncle": Bernays's mother was Freud's sister, his father the brother of Freud's wife. Bernays used the family tie as a credential all his life — and used Freud's idea of unconscious drives (not clinical technique) as the theoretical warrant for bypassing reason in persuasion.

02

Doris E. Fleischman (1891–1980)

Bernays's wife from 1922 and his equal business partner — a published feminist writer who originated many of the firm's campaigns while working behind the scenes.

03

A. A. Brill (1874–1948)

Freud's first American translator and a leading New York psychoanalyst. Consulted on the women's-smoking problem; in Bernays's own retelling, Brill framed cigarettes as "torches of freedom." No independent transcript of the consultation survives — the wording is Bernays's account.

04

Walter Lippmann (1889–1974)

Columnist and author of Public Opinion (1922); sat with Bernays on the Creel Committee. Bernays quotes him constantly — but scholar Sue Curry Jansen argues Bernays systematically distorted Lippmann, inverting a critique of irrational stereotypes into an apology for PR.

05

Gustave Le Bon & Wilfred Trotter

Le Bon's crowd psychology (Psychologie des foules) and Trotter's Instincts of the Herd in Peace and War are the two thinkers Bernays most directly cites: the public as an irrational herd that must be guided — the premise under everything he built.

06

The critics

Justice Felix Frankfurter ("professional poisoners of the public mind"); Editor and Publisher ("the young Machiavelli of our time"); Stuart Ewen (PR! A Social History of Spin, 1996); John Stauber and Sheldon Rampton; Mark Crispin Miller. The industry itself kept him at arm's length.

The machinery

Four ideas that run the modern media.

1. The engineering of consent

Bernays's name for the deliberate, planned organization of public opinion so that leaders secure public acquiescence. He defined it as "the use of an engineering approach — that is, action based only on thorough knowledge of the situation and on the application of scientific principles and tried practices to the task of getting people to support ideas and programs." In the 1947 essay he called it "the very essence of the democratic process, the freedom to persuade and suggest." The verb is doing the heavy work: consent is not spontaneous; it is manufactured the way a bridge is designed. The dark corollary, quoted in an American-government textbook written partly in reply to him: engineered consent is "difficult to distinguish in any fundamental way from the consent that supports modern totalitarian governments."

2. Public relations counsel vs. press agent

The central thesis of Crystallizing Public Opinion (1923). A press agent merely purveys news to the media; a counsel on public relations is a social-science-trained adviser who studies group psychology, builds symbolically resonant linkages between client and public, and — the key move — creates the news itself rather than chasing it. The pancake breakfast, the physician survey, the parade: none were responses to events. They were events, designed to be reported.

3. Propaganda as technique

In Propaganda (1928), Bernays uses the word as a neutral technical term for the systematic propagation of ideas. His opening argument: the "conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses" is necessary in democracy, because vast populations must cooperate smoothly — and that manipulation is inevitably performed by a small number of unseen people who understand mass mental processes. His premise, drawn from Freud, Le Bon, and Trotter: behavior is driven by unconscious desire and herd instinct, so rational persuasion is insufficient; appeals must bypass reason and engage emotion, symbolism, and group identity. (The word only hardened into a pejorative after WWI — which is why Bernays later pivoted to the euphemism "public relations" while defending the same technique.)

4. The third-party authority technique

Bernays's signature method, in his own words: "If you can influence the leaders, either with or without their conscious cooperation, you automatically influence the group which they sway." Instead of advertising a product, he mobilized independent-seeming authorities to speak for it: physicians surveyed on the hearty breakfast, dentists and medical associations, psychoanalysts, opinion leaders — plus commissioned surveys, experiments, and polls publicized as ostensibly neutral news. The Times obituary described it as his practice of favoring endorsements from experts and celebrities "to strengthen the arguments his clients wanted to make." The modern descendant is everywhere: the sponsored study, the expert panel, the influencer who "just happens" to love the product.

The curriculum

Ten modules, source-critical throughout.

Each module pairs a verifiable history with an exercise in detecting the machinery — including Bernays's own myth-making.

Module 1

The man and the myth — biography & self-invention

Vienna 1891 to Cornell 1912 to the first publicity jobs. How Bernays styled himself: "public relations counsel," the Freud connection as credential, the autobiography as PR for its author.

Exercise: compare the 1995 NYT obituary with Bernays's autobiographical claims; list what each omits. Assessment: a 300-word critical biography separating documented fact from self-mythology.

Module 2

The Freud connection — double nephew, double-edged tool

The family tree, verified precisely; the 1925 "Onkel Sigm." letter. How Bernays used Freud's idea of the unconscious — plus Le Bon and Trotter — rather than any clinical doctrine.

Exercise: match each of Bernays's cited influences to one campaign element. Assessment: short essay — "Was Bernays's Freud a theory or a brand?"

Module 3

The crucible — the Creel Committee, 1917–18

The Committee on Public Information's wartime persuasion machine; Bernays's documented post (Export Section head; Latin American Section co-head, Foreign Press Bureau). What the committee taught him about mass opinion — and how thin the documentation of his actual duties is.

Exercise: weigh what is documented against what Bernays later claimed. Assessment: a source-reliability memo.

Module 4

The first textbook — Crystallizing Public Opinion (1923)

Counsel vs. press agent; "create news, don't purvey it"; the hotel, bacon-survey, and Lithuanian cases. Jansen's charge that Bernays distorted Lippmann.

Exercise: read the opening chapters (public domain) and test one use of Lippmann against Public Opinion (1922). Assessment: argue for or against Jansen's charge in 500 words.

Module 5

The manifesto — Propaganda (1928)

Close reading of the "invisible government" opening; propaganda as neutral technique; the case that irrational masses require expert guidance. The 1928 text entered the U.S. public domain on January 1, 2024.

Exercise: paraphrase the famous opening paragraph without the words "manipulation," "invisible," or "masses" — then discuss what is lost. Assessment: attributed quote-identification quiz.

Module 6

The stunt as science — Torches of Freedom (1929)

Full reconstruction: Hill's brief, the Brill consultation, the Easter Parade, the April 1, 1929 Times story; the parallel "Reach for a Lucky instead of a sweet" and green-pack campaigns; Bernays's late-life regret narrative.

Exercise: draft the campaign as a modern PR brief, then mark every step that would now violate disclosure rules. Assessment: debate — "Did the stunt change norms, or merely surf a wave?"

Module 7

The technique lab — third-party authority & overt acts

The bacon-and-eggs physician survey; Ivory Soap contests; Dixie Cup; Light's Golden Jubilee (October 1929); Coolidge's pancake breakfast (1924); the NAACP Atlanta convention (1920).

Exercise: build a taxonomy table — technique, authority mobilized, concealment level, evidence quality. Assessment: write a modern third-party-authority brief for a public-interest client, with full disclosure.

Module 8

The darkest brief — Guatemala (1950–54)

United Fruit's PR mechanics: journalist tours, the Guatemala Newsletter to 250 journalists, the congressional Report on Guatemala. The CIA's Operation PBSuccess as a separate operation. The line between a verified propaganda role and coup causation.

Exercise: compare a Bernays-era news story with the scholarly consensus; identify the planted narrative. Assessment: essay — "What Bernays is and is not responsible for in Guatemala."

Module 9

The phrase — "The Engineering of Consent" (1947/1955)

The Annals essay's core moves; the engineering metaphor; the claim that it is "the very essence of the democratic process." The lineage forward: Chomsky & Herman's Manufacturing Consent (1988), Curtis's The Century of the Self (2002), the algorithmic feed.

Exercise: map the lineage Bernays → Chomsky/Herman → algorithmic feeds. Assessment: "Is consent engineerable? Is it still consent?" — a structured argument.

Module 10

The reckoning — critics, late life, legacy

The named critics; the industry's ambivalence; the late anti-smoking turn with Banzhaf's ASH; the 100th-birthday warning that "any dope, any nitwit, any idiot, can call him or herself a public relations practitioner."

Exercise: audit the modern media/psyops discourse — which Bernays claims are sourced, which are conspiracist inflation? Assessment: final paper — "What survives of Bernays: method, ideology, or myth?"

Source criticism

Read the legend critically.

"Bernays invented PR." False as stated. Ivy Lee opened a PR counseling office in 1904 and issued the first press release in 1906. Bernays professionalized and branded the field — theory, books, the first university course — partly through relentless self-promotion. The "father of PR" title is partly his own manufacture.

"Bernays caused the Guatemala coup." Overclaim. He ran United Fruit's U.S.-facing propaganda campaign and fed the newswires during the June 1954 coup — a verified, significant role. The coup itself was a CIA operation ordered by Eisenhower. The balanced scholarly line: United Fruit was the most important force in toppling Árbenz, and Bernays was the company's most effective propagandist.

"Torches of Freedom made all women smoke." Overclaim. Women's share of cigarette consumption rose from roughly 5% (1923) to roughly 12% (1929) as part of a decades-long trend with many causes. The parade broke the taboo on public female smoking and supplied the template for cause-linked marketing. Treat the share figures as commonly cited estimates, not hard data.

"Goebbels used Bernays's book." Single source: Bernays himself, in his 1965 autobiography — a second-hand dinner anecdote he chose to publish about himself. Historically interesting; not independent evidence of Nazi reading habits.

The wristwatch story. The claim that Bernays persuaded American men to adopt the wristwatch circulates in videos and listicles, but no reputable published source confirms it. This track excludes it as likely apocryphal.
