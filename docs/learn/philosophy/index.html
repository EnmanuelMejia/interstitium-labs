Philosophy: antiquity to the present.

The track

Not names and dates — the moves.

Most philosophy surveys teach you about philosophers. This one teaches you the moves: Parmenides's remorseless deduction that change is impossible; Zeno's paradoxes as weapons in its defense; Plato's argument that learning is recollection; Aristotle's four-cause checklist; Augustine's reclassification of evil as absence; Hume's airtight circle around induction; Kant's reversal of the mind–world relation; the categorical imperative as a consistency test; Nietzsche's diagnosis of a dying moral world; Russell's logical hygiene; Wittgenstein's demolition of the idea that meaning is a mental entity; Rawls's veil; Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain; Singer's pond. If you can reconstruct these arguments in your own words — premises, inference, weak point — you know philosophy. The names are just filing.

A note on method. Philosophy has its own folklore, and this track treats it the way every track on this site treats legend: the famous "thesis–antithesis–synthesis" triad is not Hegel's — it comes from Fichte and was popularized by Chalybäus. The Will to Power is not a book Nietzsche published — it is his sister's posthumous, doctored compilation. The atomic "swerve" is not Epicurus's idea — it appears only in Lucretius. Every correction of this kind is marked on the page. Where a detail could not be verified against a primary source (flagged in the sources section), the page paraphrases rather than quoting and says what is approximate. That discipline is not pedantry: in philosophy, the argument is the content, and a misquoted premise is a different argument.

On scope. The spine is the Western tradition, Presocratics to contemporary — because that is where the verifiable chain of argument runs longest. The final lesson opens the two great non-Western systems — Confucius and the Buddha — not as an afterthought but as independent convergences: the Buddha's no-self argument and Hume's bundle theory of the self reach startlingly similar conclusions from opposite ends of Eurasia. That comparison is left as an exercise, because it is the kind of question that teaches itself.

Chronology

Twenty-six centuries of argument.

Every date below is verifiable from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the primary sources named at the foot of this page; approximate or flagged dates are marked.

  1. c. 624–546 BCE

    Thales of Miletus — the first known thinker to seek natural rather than mythic explanations; the archē, the origin of all things, is water. Followed by Anaximander (c. 610–546; the apeiron, the boundless) and Anaximenes (c. 588–524; air, transformed by rarefaction and condensation).

  2. c. 551–479 BCE

    Confucius — the Analects: ren (benevolence) cultivated through li (ritual propriety); the five virtues ren, yi, li, zhi, xin.

  3. c. 5th c. BCE

    The Buddha — anattā (no-self): the person is five ever-changing aggregates (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness), none of them a self.

  4. fl. c. 500 BCE

    Heraclitus of Ephesus — reality is flux governed by a rational logos: "though the logos is common, the many live as if they had a private understanding"; the cosmos is "ever-living fire, kindled in measures and going out in measures."

  5. fl. c. 495–470 BCE

    Parmenides of Elea — fragment 8: Being is ungenerated, imperishable, one, continuous, indivisible, motionless; change and plurality are illusions, because what-is-not cannot be thought. The first great deductive argument in Western philosophy.

  6. c. 490–430 BCE

    Zeno of Elea — the paradoxes defending Parmenides: Achilles and the Tortoise, the Dichotomy, the Arrow — each a reductio of the commonsense picture of space and time.

  7. c. 490–420 BCE

    Protagoras — the homo-mensura: "man is the measure of all things." Preserved mainly through Plato's hostile Theaetetus; read with suspicion.

  8. c. 460–370 BCE

    Democritus of Abdera (dates approximate) — atoms (atomoi, "uncuttable") moving in the void; everything is combinations of indivisible particles. Founded by Leucippus, developed by Democritus.

  9. 470–399 BCE

    Socrates — the elenchus: cross-examination that reduces an interlocutor's thesis to contradiction. Executed in 399 BCE on charges of impiety and corrupting the young; refused escape in the Crito.

  10. c. 428–348 BCE

    Plato — the Theory of Forms; the Phaedo equality argument; the Meno slave-boy demonstration (learning as recollection); the Republic's tripartite soul and city; the cave allegory (Book VII).

  11. 384–322 BCE

    Aristotle — the four causes (material, formal, efficient, final); potentiality and actuality; the prime mover as pure actuality, moving everything as a final cause — the way a beloved inspires lovers.

  12. c. 360–270 BCE

    Pyrrho of Elis — suspension of judgment (epoché) before equipollent disagreement; tranquility said to follow like a shadow. Epicurus (341–270): pleasure as ataraxia and aponia; "when we are, death is not; when death is, we are not."

  13. c. 300 BCE

    Zeno of Citium founds Stoicism — the dichotomy of control; the four cardinal virtues (wisdom, justice, courage, temperance); cosmopolitanism.

  14. 204/5–270 CE

    Plotinus — from the One emanates Nous (Intellect), from Nous the World-Soul, from Soul matter; the soul's return (epistrophē) to the One.

  15. 354–430 CE

    Augustine — evil as privation of good (privatio boni): the way blindness is an absence of sight; the City of God (413–427).

  16. c. 872–950

    Al-Farabi, the "Second Teacher" — the philosopher-prophet-ruler of the virtuous city, a multicultural, multilingual, multireligious empire. Then Avicenna (980–1037; the Flying Man), Averroes (1126–1198), Maimonides (1138–1204; negative theology), and Aquinas (1225–1274; the Five Ways, Summa Theologiae I.2.3).

  17. 1463–1592

    The Renaissance turn: Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494; man as "maker and moulder of his own self"); Machiavelli (1469–1527; The Prince, 1513 — the verità effettuale); Montaigne (1533–1592; Essays, 1580; "Que sais-je?").

  18. 1596–1716

    The rationalists: Descartes (1596–1650; Meditations, 1641 — the cogito, the wax argument, mind–body dualism); Spinoza (1632–1677; Ethics, 1677 — Deus sive Natura); Leibniz (1646–1716 — monads, pre-established harmony, the Principle of Sufficient Reason).

  19. 1632–1776

    The empiricists: Locke (1632–1704; Essay, 1689/90 — tabula rasa; primary vs. secondary qualities); Berkeley (1685–1753 — esse est percipi); Hume (1711–1776; Enquiry, 1748 — the problem of induction; the is–ought passage, Treatise 3.1.1).

  20. 1724–1831

    Kant (1724–1804; Critique of Pure Reason, 1781/1787; Groundwork, 1785 — synthetic a priori, phenomena/noumena, the categorical imperative); Hegel (1770–1831; Phenomenology of Spirit, 1807 — the lordship–bondage dialectic).

  21. 1788–1873

    The 19th-century reaction: Schopenhauer (1788–1860; the world as Will); Kierkegaard (1813–1855; the three stages); Marx (1818–1883; the four alienations of the 1844 manuscripts); Mill (1806–1873; Utilitarianism, 1863; On Liberty, 1859 — the harm principle).

  22. 1844–1900

    Nietzsche — "God is dead" as the madman's cry (The Gay Science §125, 1882); master vs. slave morality (Genealogy, 1887); the Übermensch; the eternal recurrence.

  23. 1848–1951

    The linguistic turn: Frege (1848–1925; sense vs. reference, 1892); Russell (1872–1970; "On Denoting," 1905; the paradox); Wittgenstein (1889–1951; Tractatus, 1921 — the picture theory; Philosophical Investigations — meaning as use, language-games).

  24. 1859–1984

    Husserl (1859–1938; phenomenology, intentionality, the epoché); Heidegger (1889–1976; Being and Time, 1927 — Dasein as being-in-the-world); Sartre (1905–1980); de Beauvoir (1908–1986; The Second Sex, 1949); Camus (1913–1960); Arendt (1906–1975); Foucault (1926–1984).

  25. 1921–2002

    Rawls (1921–2002; A Theory of Justice, 1971 — the original position, the veil of ignorance); Nozick (1938–2002; Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974 — entitlement theory, the Wilt Chamberlain argument).

  26. 1946–present

    Singer (b. 1946; "Famine, Affluence, and Morality," 1972 — the shallow pond; the root of effective altruism); Chalmers (b. 1966 — the hard problem of consciousness; philosophical zombies).

Dramatis personae

Twelve minds, one long argument.

Each card names the figure's central move — the full exposition is in the arguments section below.

01

Socrates (470–399 BCE)

The elenchus: cross-examination that reduces a thesis to contradiction. Executed in 399 BCE; refused escape. His move is a method, not a doctrine — the examined life as a practice.

02

Plato (c. 428–348 BCE)

The Forms: genuine knowledge is of unchanging realities that sensible things merely participate in. The equality argument, recollection, the cave — the West's first systematic metaphysics.

03

Aristotle (384–322 BCE)

The four causes and potentiality/actuality. Every later physics, biology, and metaphysics is an argument with him — including the ones that claim to refute him.

04

Augustine (354–430)

Evil as privation of good — the hole-in-the-sock move. Married Greek philosophy to Christian theology and invented the interior self as a philosophical subject.

05

Avicenna (980–1037)

The Flying Man: self-awareness survives the blocking of every sense — the sharpest thought experiment in the medieval East–West conversation on mind and body.

06

Aquinas (1225–1274)

The Five Ways: five instances of one pattern — observed features of the world argued to be unintelligible unless anchored in something non-derivative.

07

Descartes (1596–1650)

Methodic doubt down to the single indubitable point: doubting is happening. The cogito proves a thinker exists while thinking — and almost nothing more.

08

Hume (1711–1776)

Induction is rationally unjustifiable but natural and indispensable — we live by custom. The circle is airtight; the answer is deflationary, not skeptical panic.

09

Kant (1724–1804)

The Copernican turn: objects conform to the mind's forms, not the reverse. Synthetic a priori knowledge is possible — at the price of knowing only phenomena.

10

Nietzsche (1844–1900)

"God is dead" as diagnosis, not triumph: the moral framework has become unbelievable, and nihilism is the consequence to be survived. The eternal recurrence is the test of survival.

11

Wittgenstein (1889–1951)

From the picture theory to language-games: meaning is use in a practice, not a mental entity. The private-language argument turns the search for meaning inward — and finds nothing to point at.

12

Arendt (1906–1975)

The banality of evil: enormous evil needed no demonic depth — only thoughtlessness. The explanatory burden moves from monstrosity to the failure to think.

The arguments

The moves, explained genuinely.

Original exposition throughout — not summaries of conclusions but reconstructions of the reasoning: the premises, the inference, the weak point.

1. Parmenides's block on change

Start from: you can only think about what is. To say "X changed" you would have to say X came from what-is-not — but what-is-not cannot be thought or spoken. So change, birth, death, and plurality are impossible; reality is one unchanging Being. The power is that the conclusion follows remorselessly from a premise about thought itself. Every later metaphysics — Plato's Forms, atomism, Aristotle's potentiality/actuality — is an attempt to answer him without absurdity.

2. Zeno's paradoxes as a defense, not a puzzle

Zeno is not confused about walking to the door. His point: if space and time are infinitely divisible, then motion is impossible — so since motion obviously happens, your assumptions about space and time must be wrong somewhere. Each paradox is a reductio of the commonsense picture, in service of Parmenides. The live question is whether modern calculus solves him or dissolves him.

3. Plato's equality argument (Phaedo)

We look at two sticks and judge them "equal but falling short of Equality itself." But to judge something as falling short of a standard, you must already know the standard — and you cannot have gotten the perfect standard from imperfect instances. Therefore the soul must have known the Forms before birth: learning is recollection. The argument's nerve is the "deficient likeness" move: copies can only be recognized as copies by someone who knows the original.

4. Aristotle's four causes as an explanatory checklist

Ask of anything: what is it made of (material), what is its form/pattern (formal), what brought it about (efficient), and what is it for (final). Modern science mostly dropped the fourth — but notice how much of biology ("the heart is for pumping") and all of human action still speaks final-cause language. The prime mover then answers: what keeps the whole causal order in motion without itself needing a mover? Something fully actual, with nothing left to become — which moves everything the way a beloved moves a lover: as an end, not a push.

5. The Stoic dichotomy as cognitive therapy

Epictetus's move is not "don't care" but a sorting operation: suffering comes from treating non-up-to-us things (reputation, health, outcomes) as though they were up-to-us. Restrict your prohairesis (moral choice) to judgments, impulses, desires — the only things fully yours — and externals become "preferred indifferents": fine to pursue, catastrophic to need.

6. Epicurus's death argument

Fear of death rests on imagining being dead — experiencing the state of non-experience. But experience requires a subject, and death is precisely the dissolution of the subject. So "death is nothing to us": the feared scenario is incoherent. The corollary is the positive program: since only present experience matters, maximize ataraxia — and that turns out to need very little (bread, water, friends, philosophy), which is why Epicureanism is the opposite of luxury.

7. Augustine's privation theory

The problem of evil assumes evil is a thing God made. Augustine reclassifies: evil is to good what a hole is to a sock — not a substance but a lack in a substance that ought to have it. God creates only good things; evil enters when free wills defect from the good they were made for. Whether the analogy holds for natural disasters is the live question — but the logical move is to deny the premise that evil needs a maker.

8. Aquinas's Five Ways as five instances of one pattern

Each begins from an observed feature of the world (things move, causes chain, things are contingent, things come in degrees, unintelligent things act toward ends) and argues it is unintelligible unless anchored in something that has that feature non-derivatively (unmoved, uncaused, necessary, maximal, intelligent). The crucial caveat Aquinas himself would insist on: these conclude to "what everyone calls God" only analogically — the arguments establish a metaphysical anchor, not the Trinity.

9. Descartes's cogito as a survivor, not a proof

Methodic doubt is demolition: senses deceive, dreams deceive, maybe a demon deceives. What cannot be doubted is not a grand metaphysical edifice but the single point that doubting is happening — "I think, therefore I am" survives because the attempt to doubt it confirms it. Note what it does not prove: that the "I" is an immaterial soul-substance persisting through time. That needs the later, shakier arguments of Meditations II–VI. The cogito proves a thinker exists while thinking, nothing more. Scholars cannot even agree on what Descartes's mind–body picture was — interactionism, trialism, hylomorphism — so cite the cogito only for what it establishes.

10. Spinoza and Leibniz: two ways out of dualism

Descartes left mind and body as two substances with no intelligible connection. Spinoza dissolves the problem: there is exactly one substance, Deus sive Natura ("God, or Nature"), and mind and body are two attributes of the same thing — like two sides of one coin, so their correlation needs no causal bridge. Leibniz keeps many substances — monads, simple and windowless, each mirroring the whole universe from its own perspective — with no real interaction between them, only pre-established harmony set by God. Two opposite solutions to the same problem: one substance, or infinitely many synchronized ones.

11. Hume on induction as circularity, not refutation

Hume does not say the sun won't rise tomorrow. He says: try to justify your confidence. Deductive justification is impossible (no contradiction in imagining otherwise). Probabilistic justification ("it's always worked") presupposes that past regularities predict future ones — which is the uniformity of nature, the very thing needing justification. The circle is airtight, and his answer is deflationary: we believe by custom, a kind of animal instinct — justified pragmatically, not rationally.

12. Kant's Copernican turn

Previous philosophy assumed the mind must conform to objects, and then despaired that objects-as-they-are-in-themselves are unreachable. Kant reverses it: objects conform to the mind's forms (space, time, the categories). That is what makes synthetic a priori knowledge possible — geometry is certain because space is our form of intuition. The price: we know only phenomena. The payoff: the moral law needs no empirical support; freedom, God, and immortality can be postulated by practical reason without theoretical proof.

13. The categorical imperative as a consistency test

Do not ask "what should I do?" — ask "could everyone do this, for this reason, without contradiction?" A lying promise fails: if everyone lied to get loans, the institution of promising (which the liar needs) collapses — the maxim contradicts itself universalized. The humanity formula adds the positive content: persons are ends, never tools merely. The test's bite is that it rules out exceptions for me — the exact structure of most immoral reasoning.

14. Hegel's master and slave as a parable of recognition

Self-consciousness needs another self-consciousness to confirm it — you cannot be "independent" alone. The life-and-death struggle produces a winner (lord) and loser (bondsman), but the lord's recognition comes from someone unfree, so it is worthless; meanwhile the bondsman, through work on the world, discovers his own formative power. Dependence inverts: the lord depends on the bondsman. The general lesson: identities built on domination are self-undermining; genuine recognition requires the other's freedom. (And no: "thesis–antithesis–synthesis" is not Hegel's terminology — see the corrections below.)

15. Nietzsche's hammer as diagnosis, not vandalism

"God is dead" is not atheist triumphalism — it is the madman mourning in a marketplace that does not yet understand what has happened: the Christian-moral framework that gave European life meaning has become unbelievable, and the values built on it (truth, pity, equality) are now unsupported. Nihilism is the consequence to be survived, not the doctrine to adopt. The eternal recurrence is the test for whether you have survived it: could you will this exact life, with every pain, infinitely many times? If yes, you have achieved amor fati — love of fate.

16. Russell's theory of descriptions as logical hygiene

"The present King of France is bald" looks like it refers to someone. Russell shows the grammar misleads: it is really an existential claim plus a uniqueness claim plus a predication — and it is simply false, no ghost-kings needed. The general method: regiment sentences into logical form to dissolve pseudo-problems. This is the founding move of analytic philosophy: many philosophical puzzles are confusions produced by surface grammar.

17. Wittgenstein's use-theory as anti-essentialism about meaning

The Tractatus tried to fix language's essence (picturing). The later Wittgenstein asks: what do we actually do with words? "Game" has no single defining feature — only family resemblances across language-games. Meaning is not a mental entity attached to a word; it is the word's role in a practice. The private-language argument extends this inward: a sensation-word defined by private ostension has no criterion of correct re-application, so it is not a language at all — even to the speaker.

18. Sartre's freedom as condemnation

"Existence precedes essence" means: for humans, unlike paper-knives, there is no blueprint. Every situation leaves options (even refusal), so not choosing is a choice. Bad faith is the repertoire of tricks for pretending otherwise — treating yourself as an object with a fixed nature ("I am a waiter," "I had no choice"). Anguish is the feeling of that freedom; bad faith is the flight from it. Facticity — past, body, class, situation — is inescapable and constitutes the situation freedom operates within, not an escape from it.

19. Rawls's veil as a fairness device

Moral arbitrariness — the class, talents, and family you were born into — should not determine your life chances, but any real-world chooser is biased by knowing their position. So: choose the rules as if you did not know who you would be. The maximin logic follows: you would rationally insure against being the worst-off, hence the difference principle. The veil does not prove justice; it operationalizes the intuition that fairness means impartiality.

20. Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain as a dynamic objection

Patterned theories (to each according to need, merit, equality...) describe end-states. But any end-state is a snapshot; free people transacting voluntarily will immediately move away from it. Start from any "just" distribution, let fans pay to watch Wilt play, and the pattern is upset by free exchange — so "liberty upsets patterns," and no end-state principle can survive voluntary transactions. The standard reply: justice constrains the background institutions, not every transaction.

21. Singer's pond as moral mathematics

The argument has exactly two premises: suffering is bad; if you can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable importance, you should. The pond case — you would wade in and muddy your clothes to save a drowning child — makes the second premise vivid and undeniable; then distance is shown to be morally irrelevant. The conclusion most people resist — that buying luxuries while children die of preventable causes is wrong, not merely uncharitable — follows by logic, not sentiment. The live objections: demandingness, and whether "comparable moral importance" smuggles in the conclusion.

Read the legend critically

Fourteen misreadings the textbooks won't correct.

"Protagoras meant anything goes." The homo-mensura doctrine survives almost entirely through Plato's hostile Theaetetus; as presented, it is a claim about appearance — closer to a phenomenology of perception than to "all opinions are equally true."

"The cogito proves the soul." It proves only that thinking is occurring while doubt is attempted. The immaterial, persisting res cogitans is argued separately and controversially.

"Nietzsche was a nihilist." He diagnosed nihilism as Europe's coming catastrophe and wrote against it. "God is dead" is spoken by a grieving madman, not a triumphant atheist.

"Epicurus was a glutton." Epicurean pleasure is ataraxia plus aponia — tranquility and absence of pain. The school's actual menu was barley, water, and conversation.

"Stoicism means suppressing emotions." The Stoics target false judgments ("this insult harmed me"), not feeling; the sage still feels joy, caution, and wishing — the eupatheiai.

"Hegel's dialectic is thesis–antithesis–synthesis." He never used the formula. It derives from Fichte and was popularized by Chalybäus; documented by Mueller (1958). Hegel's movement is a concept's internal contradiction generating a preserving-and-canceling unity (Aufhebung).

"Machiavelli taught evil." The Prince describes the logic of power maintenance as distinct from Christian morality — descriptive political science, whether or not one endorses the advice.

"Hume proved reason is worthless." Hume is a mitigated skeptic: induction is rationally unjustifiable but natural and indispensable. He suspends justification, not belief.

"Berkeley thought things pop out of existence unperceived." God perceives everything continuously — that is precisely the role God plays in the system, the guarantor of continuity. The view is theism-dependent by design.

"Kant said we can't know anything." Phenomena are fully real — empirically real. What is unknowable is things apart from our forms of cognition. A misreading Kant fought in his lifetime.

"The 'swerve' is Epicurus's idea." It appears only in Lucretius (De Rerum Natura 2.251–292); nothing in surviving Epicurus contains it. Attribute carefully.

"Sartre meant you can do literally anything." Facticity — your past, body, class, historical situation — is inescapable and constitutes the situation in which freedom operates.

"Arendt called Eichmann's evil ordinary or excusable." "Banal" modifies the perpetrator, not the crime: the horror is that enormous evil needed no demonic depth — only thoughtlessness.

"The is–ought gap means morality is impossible." Hume's paragraph poses the problem as a challenge to moral rationalists; it does not contain a proof that no ought follows from any is.

The curriculum

Eighteen lessons, primary texts throughout.

Each lesson pairs a teaching focus with free primary-text readings (Project Gutenberg, New Advent, marxists.org, accesstoinsight.org) and SEP reference entries — plus discussion and assessment prompts.

Lesson 1

The first question: what is everything made of?

The Milesian move from myth to physis; archē-theories (water, apeiron, air) as the birth of natural explanation.

Reading: Presocratic fragments (Gutenberg) + SEP "Presocratics." Discussion: why is "everything is water" a philosophical advance over "the gods did it"? Assessment: is an archē-theory falsifiable? Should it be?

Lesson 2

Heraclitus vs. Parmenides: flux against logic

Heraclitus's logos and unity of opposites; Parmenides fragment 8's deductive case that change is impossible — the deadlock that mothers all later metaphysics.

Reading: Heraclitus fragments + Parmenides fragment 8 (Gutenberg) + SEP "Heraclitus," "Parmenides." Assessment: reconstruct Parmenides's argument in 4 numbered premises — which premise would you deny, and what does denying it cost?

Lesson 3

Zeno and the birth of the reductio

The paradoxes (Achilles, Dichotomy, Arrow) as reductio ad absurdum in defense of Parmenides; infinite divisibility vs. motion.

Reading: SEP "Zeno's Paradoxes" + Aristotle's Physics VI excerpts. Discussion: do the paradoxes attack motion, or our concepts of space and time?

Lesson 4

Socrates: the examined life and its price

The elenchus; Socratic ignorance; the 399 BCE trial, charges, and execution; civil disobedience in the Crito.

Reading: Plato's Apology, Crito (Gutenberg) + SEP "Socrates." Assessment: debate — does a citizen ever owe obedience to an unjust verdict?

Lesson 5

Plato: Forms, recollection, and the cave

The Theory of Forms; the Phaedo equality argument; the Meno slave-boy; the tripartite soul and city; the cave allegory.

Reading: Meno, Phaedo (selections), Republic VII (Gutenberg) + SEP "Plato's Metaphysics." Discussion: who in the cave allegory are we — prisoners, escapee, or returners?

Lesson 6

Aristotle: causes, substance, and the prime mover

The four causes; potentiality/actuality; the prime mover as pure actuality and final (not efficient) cause.

Reading: Physics II, Metaphysics XII (selections) + SEP "Aristotle's Metaphysics." Assessment: apply the four causes to a smartphone, then to a human action.

Lesson 7

Hellenistic therapies: Stoics, Epicureans, skeptics

The dichotomy of control; Epicurean pleasure as ataraxia; Pyrrhonian epoché — philosophy as medicine for anxiety.

Reading: Epictetus's Enchiridion, Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus (Gutenberg) + SEP "Stoicism," "Epicurus," "Skepticism." Assessment: one-week journal experiment using the dichotomy of control; report what broke.

Lesson 8

Plotinus to Augustine: evil and the One

Emanation (One → Nous → Soul); Augustine's privation theory of evil; the two cities.

Reading: Plotinus Enneads selections, Augustine Confessions VII, City of God XIX (New Advent) + SEP "Plotinus," "Augustine." Discussion: does privation theory handle natural evil?

Lesson 9

Reason and revelation: Farabi to Aquinas

Al-Farabi's philosopher-prophet and the virtuous city; Avicenna's Flying Man; Averroes on faith and reason; Maimonides's negative theology; Aquinas's Five Ways.

Reading: Summa Theologiae I.2.3 (New Advent), Guide for the Perplexed I.50–60 (Gutenberg) + SEP "Al-Farabi," "Ibn Sina," "Aquinas." Assessment: does the Flying Man prove dualism, or only self-awareness-without-body?

Lesson 10

Descartes to Leibniz: the rationalist system-builders

Methodic doubt and the cogito's limits; mind–body dualism and the interaction problem; Spinoza's monism (Deus sive Natura); Leibniz's monads and sufficient reason.

Reading: Meditations I, II, VI, Ethics I, Monadology (Gutenberg) + SEP "Descartes' Epistemology," "Spinoza," "Leibniz." Discussion: which solves the mind–body problem better — Spinoza's one substance or Leibniz's pre-established harmony?

Lesson 11

Locke, Berkeley, Hume: the empiricist arc

Tabula rasa; primary/secondary qualities; esse est percipi; the problem of induction; the is–ought paragraph.

Reading: Locke Essay II (selections), Berkeley Principles, Hume Enquiry IV–V, XII + SEP "Locke," "Berkeley," "Hume." Assessment: write Hume's induction argument as a dilemma; then say whether you can live with custom as your only ground.

Lesson 12

Kant: the Copernican revolution in philosophy

Synthetic a priori; phenomena/noumena; transcendental idealism vs. phenomenalism; the categorical imperative's formulas.

Reading: Prolegomena (whole — it is short), Groundwork I–II (Gutenberg) + SEP "Kant's Transcendental Idealism," "Kant's Moral Philosophy." Assessment: test three of your own maxims against the universalization test.

Lesson 13

Hegel, Kierkegaard, Marx: history, existence, economy

Master–slave and recognition; the thesis–antithesis–synthesis myth; Kierkegaard's stages and the parody of Hegel; Marx's four alienations.

Reading: Phenomenology §§178–196, Fear and Trembling selections, the 1844 Manuscripts (marxists.org) + SEP "Hegel," "Kierkegaard," "Marx." Discussion: find a real relationship with the lordship–bondage structure; who does the recognizing?

Lesson 14

Mill, Nietzsche, Schopenhauer: values under pressure

The greatest happiness principle and higher pleasures; the harm principle and its "maturity" restriction; Schopenhauer's Will; Nietzsche's death of God, master/slave morality, eternal recurrence.

Reading: Utilitarianism II + On Liberty I, Genealogy I + Gay Science §125, §341 (Gutenberg) + SEP "Mill's Moral and Political Philosophy." Assessment: take the eternal recurrence test seriously for one week of your life.

Lesson 15

Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein: the linguistic turn

Sense vs. reference; definite descriptions; logical atomism and Russell's paradox; picture theory → language-games; private language.

Reading: "On Sense and Reference" (selections), "On Denoting" (1905), Tractatus Preface + Investigations §§1–43 + SEP "Frege," "Wittgenstein." Assessment: give three sentences whose surface grammar misleads about their logical form.

Lesson 16

Phenomenology and existentialism: consciousness and freedom

Intentionality and the epoché; Dasein as being-in-the-world; existence precedes essence; bad faith; the Absurd and revolt; "one becomes a woman."

Reading: Existentialism Is a Humanism, Myth of Sisyphus (selections), Second Sex Introduction + SEP "Sartre." Assessment: catch yourself in one act of bad faith this week; write it up in Sartrean vocabulary.

Lesson 17

Justice and power: Rawls, Nozick, Arendt, Foucault

The veil of ignorance and the two principles; entitlement theory and "liberty upsets patterns"; the banality of evil; disciplinary power and the Panopticon.

Reading: Theory of Justice ch. 3 (selections), Anarchy, State, and Utopia ch. 7, Eichmann in Jerusalem Postscript, "Panopticism" + SEP "Original Position," "Arendt," "Foucault." Assessment: design a classroom grading policy from behind the veil; then let Nozick attack it with Wilt Chamberlain.

Lesson 18

Consciousness, giving, and the self: Chalmers, Singer, Buddha, Confucius

The hard problem and zombies; effective altruism's shallow pond; anattā and the five aggregates; ren and li.

Reading: "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995, web), "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972, web), the Anattalakkhana Sutta (accesstoinsight.org), Analects 1–4, 12 (Gutenberg) + SEP "Zombies," "Confucius."
